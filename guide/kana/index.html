    Kana and typing

    かなと入力

    Hiragana and katakana in a few days, a Japanese keyboard on every device you own, and why romaji early on is fine.

    6 min readDay one✓ Read

    仮

    Japanese uses three writing systems at once: hiragana, the round one that does most of the grammar; katakana, the angular one for foreign words and emphasis; and kanji, the Chinese characters. Kanji gets its own chapter later. Right now it’s just the first two, and honestly kana is the easiest part of the whole language. It’s around 46 basic characters each plus a few marks, and most people have them down in a few days to a week.

    Getting kana down

    Start with hiragana, since it’s on literally every line of Japanese you’ll ever read, then do katakana. Short sessions a few times a day work better than one long miserable one. And when I say “down,” I mean you can read them, slowly, while you’re still reviewing them in Anki or Quizlet or whatever. You don’t need to be fast. Once you can read kana at all, you can start doing your vocab flashcards in kana, and from then on every card and every line you read keeps drilling them for you, so you basically don’t have to study them separately anymore.

    Katakana is going to stay slower for a while because you see it less, and that’s completely normal. I’ve been studying for years and katakana still trips me up sometimes. Reading menus, games and anything heavy on loanwords fixes it over time. I made a free Kana Trainer on this site for exactly this step if you want something to drill with.

    What about romaji?

    People ask whether it’s bad to learn words before you know all of hiragana and katakana, and I don’t think it is. If you learn sakana means fish from romaji and later see さかな, your brain goes “oh, sakana, I know that one,” and you haven’t lost anything. A lot of people start that way. The thing to avoid is staying on romaji. As soon as you can read kana, switch your flashcards, your notes and the way you type over to it, because leaning on romaji long term makes reading real Japanese slower.

    Get a Japanese keyboard on everything

    You’re going to need to type Japanese to look up words, search YouTube and eventually text people, and every device already has a free Japanese input method (an IME) built in:

    • Windows: Settings → Time & language → Language & region → Add a language → Japanese. Switch with Alt + ` or Win + Space.
    • Mac: System Settings → Keyboard → Input Sources → add Japanese – Romaji. Switch with Ctrl + Space.
    • iPhone / Android: add the Japanese keyboard in settings. You can type romaji on the normal layout or use flick, the 12-key layout where you tap a kana and swipe for the rest of its row.

    You type romaji (nihongo), it shows にほんご, Space converts it to 日本語, and Enter confirms. ん sometimes needs nn, and a small っ comes from doubling the next consonant (kitte → きって).

    If you’re wondering what Japanese people actually type with, it’s mostly romaji too. Recent surveys put it at around 94%, with kana input (a layout where every key is a kana) at about 5%. Even on phones, a DOCOMO survey of about 6,000 people found fewer than half of every age group use flick. So don’t feel like a fake typing romaji on your phone; I do it on both my PC and my phone. Flick is still fun to learn, though. I went from about 8 characters in 20 seconds to 21 in 21 with some practice, and if you want to go down the kana input rabbit hole, I made a little trainer called JIS Grind.

    Do you need to write by hand?

    It depends on your goals. Thanks to the IME, you can read, type, text and work in Japanese without ever handwriting a kanji. I can write kana and maybe 50 kanji from memory, but I can read around 2,600, and that kind of gap is pretty normal for immersion learners. That said, if you enjoy it or want to really understand how kanji break down into parts, go for it. My friend DASmallWorlds, who’s N1 and studying for the top level of the Kanji Kentei, makes a good case that writing helps you tell apart look-alikes like 待 and 持. I’d at least write out kana by hand once or twice so the shapes stick.
