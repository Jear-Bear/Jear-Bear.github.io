    Roadmap and resources

    道しるべ

    The whole guide as a checklist you can tick off, what I think about the JLPT, every resource in one place, and the other guides worth reading.

    9 min readEveryone✓ Read

    道

    Here’s everything from the guide, boiled down into stages you can tick off. Your checks are saved in this browser. The stages are rough; they overlap, and nobody fails for doing them out of order.

    The roadmap

    • Pin your why (chapter 1)
    • Read hiragana and katakana, even slowly (chapter 2)
    • Japanese keyboard on your phone and computer
    • Install Anki (or Migaku) and start Kaishi 1.5K at 5 new cards a day
    • Skim Tae Kim or Sakubi, once
    • Watch comprehensible-input videos or a slice-of-life anime with Japanese subtitles
    • Set up Yomitan + asbplayer and mine your first sentence card
    • Finish Kaishi 1.5K (or know most of it)
    • Finish your first manga volume
    • Read NHK Easy and hit only 1–2 unknown words per article
    • Play a game or visual novel in Japanese
    • A daily routine you’ve kept for a month
    • Switch to Japanese–Japanese definitions (~3,000 words)
    • Finish your first light novel
    • Enjoy a show you haven’t seen before with Japanese subtitles, few lookups
    • Pick a shadowing partner and shadow a little every week
    • Pass a round of Kotu’s pitch minimal pairs
    • Have your first real conversation (italki, Discord, VRChat, a meetup)
    • Phone and computer in Japanese
    • One hobby done fully in Japanese
    • Finish a native novel
    • Pick something that intimidates you, and finish it
    • A Japanese friend you talk to regularly
    • Help someone who’s where you were a year ago

    How long does all this take?

    Honestly, it depends on your hours, not your calendar. With a lot of time, you can get pretty decent in two or three years; SevenStop scored near-perfect on the N1 at around three. Getting near-native can take ten or twenty. The progress isn’t linear: the first couple of years build the big foundation, and the rest is fine-tuning and filling in specialized words. For me, it’s been about 4,000 hours so far, and I’m still まだまだ. (That’s literally the channel name.)

    Should you take the JLPT?

    The JLPT is the official Japanese proficiency test, from N5 (easiest) to N1. My take:

    • Take it if you plan to work in Japan, or if you’re just curious where you stand.
    • A certificate doesn’t always match ability. People pass N1 and can’t hold a conversation; people who can’t pass N3 chat with natives all day. It mostly tests reading and listening.
    • For jobs: N3 can get you started, N2 is what a lot of companies want, and N1 is mostly a flex (and a nice bump for some visas and jobs).
    • Practicalities: it’s twice a year (July and December) in most places, in person, and about $100 in the US.

    話Story time

    I failed the N2 twice before passing on my third try. Both times my weak spot was reading. So I spent the next year on web novels and visual novels, sometimes watching anime on mute with only the Japanese subtitles, plus the N2 prep videos from 日本語の森 (they teach in simple Japanese, so it’s still immersion). Then two or three full mock exams with a timer. I printed those at a konbini, accidentally in a giant size, and carried three huge JLPT packets through the streets while everyone stared. Anyway, I passed. I’m taking the N1 this December.

    About the new CEFR chart

    The JLPT now publishes a chart that converts your score to a CEFR level (the international A1–C2 scale used for English, Spanish and so on). For example, passing N3 with 110 or more counts as B1. Sounds great, but there’s a catch: the JLPT only tests what you understand, and CEFR levels also describe what you can do, like speaking and writing.

    So if you pass N3 and put “B1” on your résumé, an employer will assume you can talk at that level. My advice: put the CEFR level down once you’ve actually practiced speaking and writing enough to back it up. Until then, list the JLPT level and go get some conversation hours. The test itself hasn’t changed at all.

    Other guides worth your time

    No single guide has all the answers, including this one. These people shaped how I learn:

    • TheMoeWay: the go-to written immersion guide, with detailed setups and resources. Its chapter layout inspired this guide’s.
    • DASmallWorlds’ An Introduction to Japanese: an annotated list of the resources that took him to N1 and Kanji Kentei study. He took the structured route (Genki → Quartet, WaniKani, Bunpro, monolingual dictionaries, writing kanji by hand), and he’s very well regarded in the AJATT community. If you like structure, read it. Two lines from it I love: “the best method is the one you don’t give up on,” and “don’t skimp out on your SRS reviews.”
    • SevenStop: near-perfect N1 in about three years, and great videos on grammar, passive immersion, and getting into Japanese universities.
    • Hypothetical Tiger: immerse, make cards from immersion, rep cards, repeat until fluent. And actively do the skill you want to get better at. His AJATT updates with output sections are great to watch.
    • Wyatt (Furretar): just cracked. Mined every unknown word he ever saw.
    • Matt vs Japan: the guy who got a lot of us into immersion in the first place.

    One last thing

    If you take one thing from all fourteen chapters: it’s not a race. Every word is one more stone on your mountain. Find stuff you love, mine a little, review a little, sleep, and come back tomorrow. Make mistakes and collect the stories. Go talk to people.

    And when you hit a milestone (your first manga, your first conversation, your first time thinking in Japanese without trying), come tell us in the Discord. I want to hear about it.

    As always: keep learning, keep immersing, and keep having fun with Japanese. See you around. ✌️
