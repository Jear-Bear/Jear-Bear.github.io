    Find your why

    きっかけ

    I quit Japanese four or five times. The thing that finally made me stop quitting wasn’t a method. It was a reason. Here’s mine, and a place to pin yours.

    6 min readEveryone✓ Read

    志

    Before any tools or decks or methods, I want to talk about the thing that actually decides whether you make it: why you’re doing this.

    Everyone starts excited. The novelty of a new language carries you through kana and your first few hundred words. Then it wears off, usually somewhere in the intermediate stage, where progress gets slow and invisible. That’s where people quit. That’s where I quit, over and over.

    Mine: a host family I couldn’t talk to

    I started for anime. I stayed for a family in Morioka.

    1. High school, TexasMy high school somehow had Japanese classes, so I signed up on a whim. Genki 1, Genki 2. I placed top three at the Japan Bowl competition, which got me a trip to Japan.
    2. My first homestay, MoriokaUp in Tohoku, in the countryside. My host family didn’t speak English and I didn’t really speak Japanese. They had a whole itinerary planned, fun places, and all I could say was “this is a pen” and “where’s the toilet.” We couldn’t talk about any of it. I was so frustrated.
    3. CollegeTried AJATT, did 12 hours a day, burned out hard. Took Tobira in a college class. Worked at a Japanese grocery store and actually got to use Japanese with coworkers.
    4. Two years laterI saved up and flew back to see that same family, on my own, outside of any program. My Japanese wasn’t fluent, but it was everyday conversation. We actually talked. The smiles on their faces… I won’t forget that.
    5. Since thenMore trips. Renting a car and driving around Hokkaido. Chatting with strangers, salarymen at bars, getting interviewed by a news outlet on the street. Every one of those conversations is a payoff for all the hours at my desk. I haven’t quit since.

    All those hours sitting at your computer learning Japanese, when it turns into being there in front of people, making friends… it’s so worth it.

    — me, in a Q&A stream

    Why a reason beats motivation

    Motivation is a mood. It shows up when it wants. A reason is something you can go back to on the days you don’t feel like it.

    Here’s the funny part: early on, I didn’t have a motivation shortage, I had too much. I’d sit down and go for five or six hours, then burn out and not touch Japanese for weeks. If you’re overflowing with excitement today, that’s great. Spend some of it, and save the rest for tomorrow’s session.

    What kept me going was:

    • A real person at the end of it. For me, my host family and the friends I’ve made in Japan since.
    • Stuff I genuinely wanted to understand. Anime, horror games, my favorite YouTubers, a light novel series with 30+ volumes.
    • People to do it with. We’ll get to community later, but having study buddies changes everything.

    Pin yours

    Write down why you’re learning Japanese. Be specific. “To be fluent” is a goal, not a reason. “To understand my favorite streamer without subtitles,” or “to talk to my partner’s grandma,” or “to read the manga that hasn’t been translated yet” is a reason.

    It’ll show up on the guide’s front page every time you come back. On a bad day, read it. Then do ten minutes of something you enjoy in Japanese. That’s a win.

    “Fluent” is a moving target

    One more thing before we start. People throw around “fluent” like it’s a finish line. It isn’t. To my parents, I’m basically a native speaker. To a Japanese person, I’m a foreigner with pretty good Japanese. By my own perfectionist standards, I’m terrible. You just can’t win.

    So don’t make fluency the goal. Make the goal doing the thing your why is about. Watching the anime. Talking to the family. Reading the book. You’ll be surprised how early you get to start doing it, badly, and how much fun “badly” is.

    If you need a definition anyway: in this guide, when I say fluent I roughly mean CEFR B2, where you can follow most native content and hold your own in a normal conversation.

    要TL;DR

    • Motivation comes and goes. A specific reason sticks around.
    • Too much motivation burns you out too. Short, consistent sessions win.
    • Pin your why above, and come back to it on bad days.
