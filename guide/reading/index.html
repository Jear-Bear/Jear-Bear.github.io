    Reading

    読書

    The fastest way to grow your vocabulary, and the skill I neglected the longest. From graded readers to Natsume Sōseki, plus my manga picks for every level.

    13 min readEveryone✓ Read

    読

    A while back someone told me I just needed to read more. So I went to the library, grabbed the first Japanese book I could find, opened it, and… struggled. No matter how many lookups I did, I was completely lost.

    Years later, reading is the thing I spend most of my Japanese time on: at least three hours a day this year, with a goal of 1,000 hours and 30 books. Here’s how I got from that library book to here.

    Why reading matters so much

    At my one-year AJATT update, I noticed my listening was way ahead of my reading, vocabulary and grammar. I’d failed the JLPT N2 twice, and both times my weak spot was reading. Listening was fine because I love anime and dramas (I pushed Solitary Gourmet on everyone).

    Reading is information-dense. An hour of a light novel puts way more words in front of you than an hour of anime, and you can slow down for the hard parts. Once I made reading my main thing, my vocabulary started shooting up, and weirdly, speaking got easier too. More words in your head means more words that come out.

    The ladder

    Beginner: build confidence and consistency

    • Graded readers: books written for learners, in levels. Free ones from Tadoku (NPO 多言語多読); White Rabbit Press sells good sets too.
    • NHK News Web Easy: real news rewritten in simple Japanese for kids and learners. A great N4–N3 bridge.
    • Manga with furigana: Yotsuba&! is the classic first manga. LivingJapanese has a free reading pack for volume 1 (an Anki deck with every word and sentence), which makes it so much more manageable.

    Intermediate: manage difficulty, follow your interests

    A good benchmark for this stage: you hit one or two unknown words per NHK Easy article, and slice-of-life manga feels approachable.

    • Read about your hobbies. I love weightlifting and cooking, so I switched my recipes and workout plans to Japanese and started watching a weightlifting anime and a cooking anime. My third hobby turned out to be “why do Japanese words work like this?”, so I’d Google it in Japanese and read blog posts by natives.
    • Slice-of-life manga, lots of it. Then shōnen: Jujutsu Kaisen and Dandadan are surprisingly approachable.
    • Light novels: また、同じ夢を見ていた and 君の膵臓をたべたい by Sumino Yoru (around N3 and up), コンビニ人間 (Convenience Store Woman).
    • Visual novels: reading, but it feels like playing a game. My stamina lasts way longer with these than with a plain book.

    Advanced: stamina and taste

    • NHK News (the adult version). Binge a topic: I read a whole run of articles about bear attacks in Hokkaido and came out knowing a ton of nature and ecosystem words.
    • Novels. I’m a murder-mystery nerd: Ayatsuji Yukito’s 館 series (十角館の殺人), a Japanese translation of Murder on the Orient Express.
    • The classics. Natsume Sōseki: 坊っちゃん, 三四郎, 吾輩は猫である, こころ. I tried 坊っちゃん on stream and it took me 30 minutes to get through half of the first page. One day.
    • Long series. Most of my reading this year has been 本好きの下剋上 (Ascendance of a Bookworm). 30+ volumes, a bit slow-paced, and great. Next up is 86, for a whole new pile of military vocab.

    試Tip: audiobook + text

    I read most EPUBs in the ッツ reader with the audiobook synced, so the text highlights as it’s read aloud. It helps me keep pace and read faster. I still do plenty of raw reading so I don’t lean on it too hard.

    Tricks that make reading feel doable

    “Just read more” is true, and also useless when you’re looking up every other word and you’re fried by page two. These are what actually made it click for me:

    1. Anchor on the nouns. In English, nouns jump out at you. In Japanese they blend into the line. Spot the people, places and things first, and the verbs and adjectives fall in around them. (I got this one from MoeWay.)
    2. Chunk it. Don’t stare at a whole paragraph. Take one phrase or sentence at a time, like a puzzle.
    3. Don’t fear the kanji. A scary kanji is usually a few parts you’ve seen before. Chapter 8 goes into this.
    4. Use furigana as a crutch, not forever. It’s great confidence early on. Wean yourself off it little by little.
    5. Read the summary in English first. Knowing who’s who and what’s going on gives you a scaffold to hang new words on. It’s not cheating, it’s setting the stage. I’m doing this with the Japanese translation of Murder on the Orient Express.
    6. Let repetition do its thing. 〜なければならない, 〜なくてはいけない and 〜ないといけない all mean “have to,” and after enough reading your eyes just skim them the way you skim “I gotta get outta here” in English.
    7. Keep it fun. The moment reading turns into a chore, you’ll stop. Read what you want to read, not what you think you should.

    Your first novel

    My first full novel was また、同じ夢を見ていた. Here’s what made it doable:

    • Check the coverage first. On jpdb I only start a book once I know about 95–98% of its words. At that point most lookups are names, rare words, and the occasional really useful one.
    • Raise the coverage before you open it. For harder books I watch the anime or movie adaptation first and study the book’s vocab list, so the number creeps up to where reading won’t be constant interruptions.
    • Picture it, don’t translate it. When a line doesn’t click, I close my eyes and picture the scene straight from the Japanese. Japanese → image, not Japanese → English → image.
    • Check yourself after. When I finished, I read a Japanese reader’s blog post about the ending to see if my take matched theirs. It did, mostly. (I completely missed a pun on a character’s name. Next time.)

    精読, 速読, 多読: it’s a progression, not a debate

    Scroll r/LearnJapanese long enough and every reading method becomes an argument. Intensive vs extensive, flow vs analysis. I don’t think it’s a debate. You cycle through all three:

    • 精読 (seidoku), intensive reading: slow and analytical. Look up the grammar, parse the sentence, mine. Most important as a beginner, useful forever. It builds accuracy.
    • 速読 (sokudoku), speed reading: reading at a set pace so you react instead of translate. It helped my perfectionism a lot. It builds speed.
    • 多読 (tadoku), extensive reading: tons of reading, minimal lookups, flow first. A natural SRS, since you see words again and again. But it only works if you understand most of what you’re reading. It builds endurance.

    One more thing: stopping constantly breaks your flow, but ignoring confusion stops your growth. My balance: do intensive reading on content you mostly understand, and go with the flow (fewer lookups) on stuff that’s a bit above you.

    My manga picks, by level

    I only list manga I’ve actually read. (So no Death Note or One Piece yet. I’m working on it.)

    • Yotsuba&!よつばと！

      The gateway drug. Kid protagonist, simple speech, everyday words, full furigana.

    • Komi Can’t Communicate古見さんは、コミュ症です。

      Absolute cinema. Furigana throughout, and the cast keeps growing, so you get tons of speech styles.

    • Mieruko-chan見える子ちゃん

      Slice of life with horror. Big visual panels break up the text. I read it around N3 and it didn’t feel like a kids’ manga.

    • Karada Sagashiカラダ探し

      Gory, fast, modern high-school setting. The time-loop plot is basically free SRS.

    • The Dangers in My Heart僕の心のヤバイやつ

      Slow-burn romcom in a middle school, full of the protagonist’s inner monologue.

    • You and I Are Polar Opposites正反対な君と僕

      One of my favorites. Natural romance dialogue and a lot of modern slang about hair, makeup and nails.

    • Dandadanダンダダン

      Every human on earth should read it. Yokai, aliens, urban legends; fast and funny enough to carry you through the hard bits.

    • Boukyaku Battery忘却バッテリー

      Baseball with an amnesia plot, so they re-explain the basics. Great even if you don’t like sports.

    • Golden Boyゴールデンボーイ

      Cursed. Older readers only. The humor hits harder in Japanese. I’m not going to cope about this one.

    • Hunter × HunterHUNTER×HUNTER

      Famous for gargantuan text walls, and invented terms like 念 that mean something different from real life. I’ve watched the anime five times.

    • Bakiグラップラー刃牙

      If you’ve ever wanted testosterone in manga form. As a gym guy, this does wonders for me.

    • JoJo’s Part 3: Stardust Crusadersジョジョの奇妙な冒険

      Lots of speech styles across the main cast, and lots of new words explaining Stand powers.

    • Hajime no Ippoはじめの一歩

      I quit it the first time and came back when I was ready. Training and boxing vocab, and the old coaches talk differently. Training, fighting, getting stronger… it’s just like AJATT.

    When reading starts to feel easy

    This year there have been reading sessions where I go thirty minutes, sometimes a couple of hours, without thinking in English at all. It’s an amazing feeling, and it’s also a sign: time to pick harder books. The hard stuff is where the unknown words are, and that’s where you learn the most.

    And one last thing: if you genuinely want to read something, don’t let the difficulty stop you. Take it bit by bit, be consistent, and close the book when you’re tired. Even native speakers do that.

    要TL;DR

    • Reading grows vocabulary fastest. It’s the skill most immersion learners neglect.
    • Graded readers → NHK Easy → furigana manga → light novels / VNs → whatever you want.
    • Cycle 精読, 速読 and 多読. They support each other.
    • Read about your hobbies. When it feels comfy, pick something harder.
