    Make your life Japanese

    日本語で暮らす

    The “all the time” part of AJATT: switching your world over a little at a time, finding Japanese hours in a busy day, and why you don’t have to move to Japan.

    7 min readIntermediate → Advanced✓ Read

    暮

    The “all the time” in All Japanese All The Time is about slowly letting Japanese take over your environment, and the key word there is slowly. It shouldn’t be like flipping a switch. When I was three months into Japanese, I changed my iPhone to Japanese because, well, it’s AJATT, everything I look at has to be Japanese. I was working at a restaurant at the time and tried to add my manager as a contact, tapped the wrong option in a menu I couldn’t read, and accidentally shared my location with him forever. It was really awkward the next day. My devices are all in Japanese now and it’s great, but that’s because my reading is at a comfortable level. Before that, it’s better to switch things over bit by bit as you learn the words for them. Here’s roughly the order I went in:

    • YouTube’s interface language → Japanese. Follow Japanese creators so the algorithm feeds you more.
    • Music: find Japanese artists you love (easy rabbit hole).
    • Netflix and streaming profiles → Japanese audio and subtitles.
    • A Japanese Twitter/X or Instagram feed. Follow Japanese accounts, interact, let the algorithm do the rest.
    • Games in Japanese (chapter 7).
    • Phone and computer → Japanese, once you can read comfortably. Every button becomes mini input.
    • One hobby, fully in Japanese: recipes, workouts, a craft, whatever you do anyway.

    Do your hobbies in Japanese

    Once you get into the more advanced stages, you stop feeling progress from one day to the next, and I think the most important thing then is to pursue your hobbies and interests, as a human being, in Japanese. I cook from Japanese recipes and follow workout plans in Japanese, and when I have some weird question about why a word works the way it does, I Google it in Japanese and end up reading blog posts by native speakers. By doing that, your life slowly converts to Japanese. You stop needing your native language for much besides work and friends, so you get more hours of immersion without really trying, and you end up enjoying the language more as a person. It also helps to enjoy the same story across the visual novel, manga, anime and sometimes the unfortunate live-action, since each version gets easier.

    Following Japanese accounts about your hobbies is also how you keep up with slang, because the language changes faster than any textbook can, and most new slang starts on social media. A big one lately is the suffix 〜界隈 (kaiwai), which turns any tiny habit into a “community,” like 風呂キャンセル界隈 for the people skipping their bath tonight. I go through the trend rankings every year, and it’s a fun way to pick up words your textbook will never have.

    Finding the hours

    Between a full-time job and the channel, what made it possible for me was to stop looking for “study time” and start filling the gaps in my day. On my lunch break I drive five minutes to a Costco near work, pull out my laptop, put on headphones and watch Japanese YouTube with Migaku for an hour. On the commute it’s a podcast, or audio from an anime I’ve already seen. During downtime at work I’ll read an NHK article or a few pages of a book on my phone, and at the gym I have Japanese in my ears between sets. Then at home I put on one or two episodes of something easy, maybe read ten pages of a novel, and mine five to ten words from whatever I watched.

    Look for the moments where you’d otherwise be doomscrolling; even thirty minutes a day turns into hundreds of hours a year. Then prioritize the rest of your life, your health, sleep and friends, because you don’t want Japanese to become the reason your life feels hard.

    You don’t need to live in Japan

    It’s kind of a myth that you have to live in Japan to get really good. That used to be true, but Japanese media has never been more accessible, and as a pretty socially introverted guy, I’d honestly rather have a mountain of input tools at home than be dropped in Japan with zero speaking experience.

    That said, going is the best reward there is. On my most recent trips, speaking felt so much easier than the year before, mainly because my vocabulary had grown from all the reading, and I didn’t really have to fall back on English. Every hour at the desk shows up in those conversations. One trick if you go and can’t read the menu: look at the server and ask おすすめは何ですか？ (what do you recommend?). You get a real conversation out of it, they pick something great, and you never had to read a single kanji.
