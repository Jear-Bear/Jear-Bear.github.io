“Just immerse, bro.” You’ve probably seen it on r/LearnJapanese or heard it from some dude in a Discord server, and at this point it’s basically the holy gospel of language learning. And it’s mostly right! But it took me years of setbacks and figuring things out the hard way to learn the parts people don’t usually say out loud, and that’s what this chapter is about.

Picking what to immerse in

The most important skill in immersion is choosing what to immerse in. If you dive into something too hard, you run into way too many unknown words and your mental stamina just dwindles. If it’s too easy, you don’t grow. Here’s roughly what 80% comprehension feels like:

For watching stuff, anything above about 70% is enough to enjoy it, and at 85 or 90% you’re just chilling and picking up the rest from context. But I balance it against interest (apologies to any med students, but a super easy medical textbook in Japanese still loses to a horror story for me) and difficulty, which works like the gym: lift the same weight forever and you don’t get stronger. Comprehension you can check on jpdb.io for basically any anime, manga, novel or game before you commit to it.

Something that helped me a lot this year was researching stuff before starting it. Even if something is “goated for immersion,” I ask myself whether I could sit down for dozens of hours with it and come out happy, and that little bit of research gets me hyped for it.

The flip side is the comfort trap. If you can sit through something like Komi Can’t Communicate and fully enjoy it, that’s a sign to level up, not to stay there. I keep a list of harder stuff I genuinely want to watch, like Psycho-Pass and Detective Conan, and move up to it. As my swim coach used to say, you’re not going to grow if you don’t put yourself under pressure.

Active and passive immersion

Active immersion is when Japanese has your full attention: Japanese subtitles on, pausing and replaying lines, looking things up, mining. It’s the most bang for your buck, and it’s tiring. Passive immersion is Japanese playing while you do something else, and it gets a bad rep, partly because of people like me blasting Japanese radio while scrolling Instagram in English and calling it immersion.

My friend SevenStop explained it in a way that changed how I do it. Passive immersion isn’t about not paying attention, it’s about paying attention while you do something that runs on autopilot, like driving, mowing the lawn or making breakfast, not homework or anything that needs your brain. It also depends on your level. As a beginner, the best passive listening is stuff you’ve already watched actively, like ripping the audio from an anime episode and putting it on your phone. Once your listening is around intermediate, you can learn a ton from new podcasts and YouTube in the background, the same way you’d follow an English podcast in the car.

Subtitles

Japanese subtitles give you more context, confirm what you heard, and let you look things up and mine in real time (chapter 4 has how to get them on basically anything). English subtitles mostly turn the Japanese into background noise. I binged all of Free!, the swimming anime, with English subtitles, told myself it was still exposure, and came out knowing the character names. What actually helped was watching shows I couldn’t wait for in English first and coming back to them in Japanese later, once I knew the plot.

The Ultimate Japanese Immersion Tier List

Anime is fine, anime-only isn’t

A lot of people worry that learning from anime will make them sound like an anime character. SevenStop spent his first year and a half or two immersing mostly in anime and doesn’t sound weird at all, because probably 95% of even anime is just normal Japanese. If a character says something wild, ask yourself whether a normal person would say that, and branch out into vlogs, dramas and podcasts. That matters for vocabulary too: if I only watched detective shows, I’d miss politics, fantasy words, cooking, all of it.

How to ACTUALLY Learn Japanese from Anime

Video games count

I used to feel so guilty every time I played a game. “You haven’t finished your Anki. You could be reading.” Then I had the shocking revelation that games come in Japanese. So I tested nine of them for an hour each, tracking how much Japanese I saw, how much I understood and how many words I mined:

A visual novel throws around 25,000 characters an hour at you, while Resident Evil 3 gives you about 9,000, because you’re busy running from zombies. Visual novels are the gold mine, but I’m an open-world junkie, and Cyberpunk 2077 has Japanese everywhere, from phone calls to random people on the street. And even DayZ, the worst one on my list, beats lying in bed thinking about immersing.

To mine from games, I use Game Sentence Miner with OBS. GSM grabs the text off the screen, Yomitan works on it, and your cards come with a screenshot and the voice line. Set OBS’s replay buffer to about three minutes, and mute your mic and desktop audio in OBS so your Discord call doesn’t end up on your flashcards.

Game Sentence Miner running over a visual novel, with the text hooker window and a Yomitan popup

One more trick: binge one genre for a while. I went through a run of horror detective games that all share the same words about ghosts, curses and crime scenes, and got a lot of “aha” moments when they came up again in the next one.
