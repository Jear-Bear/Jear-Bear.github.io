If I could go back and redo my first six years, here’s the honest truth: the level I reached after Genki 1, Genki 2 and Tobira could’ve been reached way faster with a good starter deck and a quick read through a grammar guide.

So here’s the starter kit I’d use today. It’s short on purpose. The goal is to get you to real, native Japanese content as fast as possible, because that’s where you actually learn.

Step 1: a starter deck (Kaishi 1.5K)

A starter deck is a set of flashcards with the most common words in Japanese, in order. The 1,500 most common words cover a surprisingly huge chunk of everyday Japanese, so they’re the most bang for your buck you’ll ever get.

My pick is Kaishi 1.5K. It’s free, it’s modern, every card has a natural example sentence and native audio, and it teaches the kanji of each word as you go. (Back in the day I did 3,000 words of the Core 2K/6K deck, which also works, it’s just older and longer.)

  • Start at 5 new cards a day for the first week or two. I know that sounds slow. Chapter 5 shows you why it isn’t.
  • Then go up to 10 or 15 if your reviews feel easy. Never jump to 50. (Ask me how I know.)
  • When a card has a word you already know from anime or wherever, mark it easy and move on.
A Kaishi 1.5K card in Anki: the front, and the back with the sentence and audio

注Don’t get addicted to decks

After my first starter deck, I got hooked. I downloaded a Core 10K, then a 10,000-word deck of just nouns. Soon I had Anki open for two hours a day and only watched 20 minutes of anime. That’s backwards. A starter deck is a springboard, not the pool. Once you know most of the words in it, your time should shift to immersion.

Step 2: skim a grammar guide (once)

You don’t need to memorize grammar before you start. You need a rough map of how Japanese sentences work: particles, verb forms, the te-form, polite vs casual. Then immersion fills in the details.

  • Tae Kim’s Guide to Japanese: the classic. Free, example-heavy.
  • Sakubi: shorter and punchier, made for immersion learners.

Read through one of them, maybe not even every chapter, at whatever pace feels fine. You won’t remember most of it. That’s the point. When you hit ～ている in an anime later, a little bell will go off, and context will do the rest. Chapter 9 is all about learning grammar this way.

What about textbooks?

Genki and friends aren’t evil. Genki 1 genuinely helped me lock in kana and basic conjugations, and the structure is nice if you like structure. My friend DASmallWorlds went Genki → Quartet → native material and got to N1, so it clearly works.

The trap is what I call textbook jail. I loved the feeling of progress from Genki so much that I went straight into another textbook, then another, and never felt “ready” to move on. Meanwhile, Genki 2 was teaching me words like 地球温暖化 (global warming) before I could hold a basic conversation, and sentences like “I gave my pet cobra a bath in the shower when it was cloudy.” When would you ever say that?

If you use a textbook, go through it once and get out. Don’t do every workbook exercise. Move on to graded readers and easy content and let those reinforce it.

Step 3: start immersing (earlier than you think)

“When can I start watching stuff?” is the question I get the most. Honestly: whenever. But earlier means harder. Here’s why:

Bob and Ross can both technically “watch anime.” Bob is going to pause every few seconds. Ross is going to find a perfect flashcard sentence every minute. Neither is wrong, but Ross is having way more fun.

Personally, I switched from grinding words to mostly immersing at around 3,000 known words. With Kaishi 1.5K done, you can comfortably start with easy content: slice-of-life anime, comprehensible-input YouTube, graded readers. We’ll pick content properly in chapter 6.

話Story time

Right after Genki 2, I tried jumping into some of my favorite anime. Way too hard. So I went back and did 3,000 words of a core deck, suspending the cards I already knew from the textbook. Then I went back to the same anime and suddenly it was watchable. Same show, same me, 3,000 more words.

The order, on one screen

  1. Kana (chapter 2): a few days.
  2. Install Anki + the starter deck (chapter 4): one evening.
  3. Kaishi 1.5K at 5–15 new cards a day, while you:
  4. Skim Tae Kim or Sakubi, once.
  5. Start easy immersion alongside the deck. Comprehensible input, slice of life, graded readers.
  6. Start mining your own cards from what you watch and read (chapter 5). Your starter deck winds down, your mined deck takes over.
