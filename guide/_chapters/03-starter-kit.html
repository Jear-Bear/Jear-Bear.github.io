If I could redo my first six years, I’m pretty confident that the level I reached after Genki 1, Genki 2 and Tobira could have been reached way faster with a good starter deck and one quick read through a grammar guide. So this chapter is short on purpose. The whole point of a starter kit is to get you to real, native Japanese as quickly as possible, because that’s where most of the learning happens.

A starter deck

A starter deck is a set of flashcards with the most common words in Japanese, roughly in order, and it’s the most bang for your buck you’ll ever get out of flashcards. I should have used one way earlier instead of random decks, like the 10,000-word deck of just nouns I downloaded early on, where half the words were barely common at all.

The one I’d point you to now is Kaishi 1.5K. It’s free and modern, every card has a natural example sentence and native audio, and it’s what SevenStop recommends too. I did about 3,000 words of the older Core 2K/6K deck back in the day, but a lot of my friends have done Kaishi and liked it.

Start at five new cards a day for the first week or two. I know that sounds slow, and chapter 6 explains why it isn’t. Go up to ten or fifteen if your reviews feel easy, and please don’t jump to fifty. If a card has a word you already know from anime or wherever, just mark it easy and move on.

A Kaishi 1.5K card in Anki: the front, and the back with the sentence and audio

One warning: decks are kind of addictive. At one point I had Anki open for two hours a day and was watching about twenty minutes of anime, which is completely backwards. A starter deck is a springboard, and once you know most of it, your time should shift to immersion.

Skim a grammar guide, once

You don’t need to memorize grammar before you start. You just need a rough map of how Japanese sentences work: particles, the main verb forms, the te-form, polite versus casual. Then immersion fills in the details. Tae Kim’s Guide to Japanese is the classic and it’s free, and Sakubi is shorter and made with immersion learners in mind. Literally just scroll through one of them at whatever pace feels fine. You won’t remember most of it and that’s fine. The idea is that when you run into ～ている in an anime later, a little bell goes off, and context does the rest. Chapter 10 is all about learning grammar that way.

What about textbooks?

Genki and friends aren’t evil. Genki 1 was honestly worth it for me for kana and the basic conjugations, and DASmallWorlds went from Genki to Quartet to native material and reached N1, so it clearly works.

The trap is what I call textbook jail. I loved the feeling of progress from Genki 1 and 2 so much that I jumped straight into another textbook, Tobira. Then I’d go back to chapters I’d already done and realize I was forgetting things, so I never felt ready to move on. Meanwhile Genki 2 was teaching me words like 地球温暖化 (global warming) and 地理 (geography) before I could form a basic sentence, and the practice sentences were things like “I gave my pet cobra a bath in the shower when it was cloudy.” When would you ever say that? What I should have done was finish the textbook once, drop it, and go to graded readers, slice-of-life anime and easy YouTube to reinforce what I’d learned.

Start immersing earlier than you think

The question I get the most is when you can start watching stuff, and my honest answer is whenever you want. The catch is that earlier means harder, because you’ll be looking up almost everything:

Bob is pausing every few seconds while Ross keeps finding great flashcard sentences. Neither is doing it wrong, Ross is just having more fun. Right after Genki 2 I tried jumping into some of my favorite anime and it was way too hard for me, so I went back and did a core deck, suspending the cards I already knew from the textbooks. When I went back to the same anime afterwards it actually felt watchable. I switched from mostly grinding words to mostly immersing at around 3,000 known words, but you can start with easy stuff (slice of life, comprehensible-input YouTube, graded readers) well before that. Chapter 7 is about picking content.

The order

  1. Kana (chapter 3), which takes a few days.
  2. Install Anki and the starter deck (chapter 5), which takes one evening.
  3. Kaishi 1.5K at five to fifteen new cards a day, and while you do that:
  4. Skim Tae Kim or Sakubi, once.
  5. Start easy immersion alongside the deck.
  6. Start mining your own cards from what you watch and read (chapter 6). The starter deck winds down and your mined deck takes over.
