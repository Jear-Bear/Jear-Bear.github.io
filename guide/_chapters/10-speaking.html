For years I told myself that if I immersed enough, I’d just magically be able to speak one day. That’s not quite how it works. Immersion gives you the words and the feel, and it makes speaking way easier. But speaking is its own skill, and the only way to get better at speaking is to speak.

When should you start?

Here’s the line I draw:

  • Talk to yourself early. Shadowing, narrating your day, thinking in Japanese (chapter 9). Start whenever you like. It can’t hurt you.
  • Talk to people once you understand basic daily conversation. Not when you’re perfect or fluent. When you can follow what the other person says and have something to say back.

Why wait at all? Because forcing it from day one goes badly. When I was early on, I downloaded HelloTalk, flocked straight to natives, and Google Translated everything I wanted to say. I couldn’t understand their replies, and I didn’t understand my own messages either. That’s not practice, that’s incomprehensible output. And talking a lot before you’ve heard a lot bakes in weird habits.

話Story time: the 2.5-hour meeting

I used to work at a Japanese supermarket. One day the store owner gave my non-employee friends the employee discount at the food court. Cool, I thought, we’re just that tight. Next day my manager, Yamamoto-san, calls me into the office: “You know stealing is bad, right?”

Early on I’d picked up a habit of tacking なんです / なんですか onto the end of everything, because longer sentences sounded more fluent to me. So instead of “Yes, of course,” what came out was basically “Oh, is it?” What should’ve been a five-minute talk turned into two and a half hours, including a free Japanese lesson. (Yamamoto-san, if you’re reading this, sorry I never came back to work.)

Once you do start talking to people, output and input feed each other. My friend SevenStop put it nicely: when you speak, you notice things you don’t know how to say. Then in your immersion you suddenly notice how natives say them. That loop is gold.

Sound natural: shadowing

Shadowing is listening to native audio and repeating it right after, copying the rhythm, intonation and pitch as closely as you can. You don’t even need to understand every word at first. It’s karaoke for your mouth.

  • Pick a shadowing partner: one YouTuber, streamer or podcaster whose voice you like and would want to sound like someday. Sticking to one person gives you a consistent target. Mine is a horror-game Let’s Player; he’s basically my language parent.
  • Short clips. One or two sentences. Play, pause, repeat. Loop it.
  • Slow it down, then speed it up. Start at 0.5× (even 0.25×), then work up to 1×.
  • Chorusing takes it further: record yourself saying the line, layer your recording over the native one, and keep redoing it until they line up.

Japanese isn’t one smooth, Eminem-rap-god stream of sounds. There are pauses and beats, just like in English when someone’s making a point. Shadowing teaches you where they go.

Pitch accent: learn to hear it

Japanese words have a melody. Each mora (roughly, each kana) is high or low, and the pattern can change meaning: 箸 (chopsticks), 橋 (bridge) and 端 (edge) are all はし. Tap through some words:

How much should you care? Learn the basic patterns, train your ear, and shadow. You don’t need to memorize the pitch of every word (come on, that’s a lot of work). Once you know there’s a pattern to listen for, you start hearing it everywhere, and shadowing makes your mouth follow.

  • Train your ear with Kotu’s minimal pairs test: it plays two words that only differ in pitch, and you pick which is which. Free and genuinely humbling.
  • Try the pitch accent tool on this site too.
  • Get your pitch dictionary into Yomitan (chapter 4) so every lookup shows the pattern.
  • For the nerds: Praat is a free tool linguists use. Record yourself, open a native clip next to it, and compare the pitch lines. The first time I did おはようございます I jumped way too high on the よ.

Six tricks that keep a conversation alive

I’ve spent thousands of hours talking with Japanese people on italki, on Discord and in VRChat. These are the six things I do over and over:

  1. 相槌 (aizuchi): little reactions while they talk, like うんうん, へぇ〜, そうなんだ. In English we’re taught to stay quiet until it’s our turn. In Japanese, silence reads as “I’m not listening.”
  2. Filler: えーと, あの, なんか. My business classes trained me to cut every “um.” In Japanese it’s the opposite: natives use filler constantly to buy thinking time.
  3. Parroting: repeat their key word back with a rising tone. They say they ate サーターアンダギー in Okinawa; you say 「サーターアンダギー？」. It shows you understood, invites them to tell you more, and buys you time.
  4. Bridging: change topics politely with そういえば, ちなみに or 話変わるけど. Jumping from their passion straight to “do you like sushi?” feels like you weren’t listening.
  5. Rare or old-school words: drop one at the right moment and you get a laugh and a whole new conversation. Better yet, ask, 「何かレアな日本語、教えてください！」. (There’s a time and place. I once called someone ボンキュッボン in VRChat. Luckily it was a dude. Do not try that on a stranger in real life. Also, apparently nobody says it anymore.)
  6. Gesture maxing: be a little funny. You’re a foreigner speaking Japanese; lean into it. Take the filter off. When you make mistakes in front of people, they repeat what you said the right way, and that’s free corrections.
How to Keep Conversations Going in Japanese

Social anxiety, in two languages

This one’s personal. Talking to strangers is hard for me in English. Add a language I’m still learning and it used to be a total disaster. Here’s what helped.

Use the confidence test

The usual advice is “just do it scared.” Nobody explains why that works. My version: ask yourself, if I were the most confident person in the world, would I do this? If yes, I go do it, terrified. My fight-or-flight kicks in, I hate awkward silence, so I fill it, and it almost always works out.

Don’t be too calculated

I went through a phase of watching social-skills YouTube gurus, and got so analytical that people were visibly uncomfortable talking to me. Be a decent human being, use a little common sense about who you’re talking to, and then just talk like a normal person.

It’s about having a good conversation more than having a native conversation.

— me

A conversation isn’t a math equation with one right answer. It’s two people trying to vibe. I’d rather connect with someone using imperfect Japanese than have a flawless, forgettable conversation where all they remember is “that white guy had pretty good grammar.” And if you don’t vibe, everyone moves on with their lives. That’s all it is.

Where to actually practice

  • Tutors on italki. One-on-one, pay per lesson, pick a teacher by price, style and dialect. I book lessons after my day job. A good teacher who isn’t afraid to correct you is huge. (Past sponsor, and I still use it.)
  • Language exchange on Discord. There are big English–Japanese exchange servers. I’ve got two or three Japanese friends I call every week or so; we switch between English and Japanese.
  • Local meetups. Most cities have Japanese meetups. I went to them back in Texas and met people working at Japanese companies.
  • VRChat. Seriously. There are Japanese worlds full of people happy to chat, and the anonymity takes the pressure off.
  • AI chatbots. I’m all for them; they’re language models, and language is literally what they’re best at. Use them for explanations and practice when you can’t find a human. Just don’t become over-reliant: they adapt to your level and won’t push you the way real people will.
