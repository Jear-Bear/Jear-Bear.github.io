I’ve quit Japanese four or five times. The longest break was about four months, and every single day of it, half of me was going “dude, you have to study, you’re getting worse,” while the other half was going “I hate Japanese, I’m so burned out, I’m so tired of this.” It was honestly painful. This chapter is the stuff I wish I’d known before each of those quits.

Know your burnout signs

In the past I wasn’t good with burnout at all. I’d wake up one day and loathe even looking at a Japanese character, and then just stop. These days I’ve gotten a bit better at noticing how I feel right before it happens. For me the biggest sign is splurging: I get a burst of energy and do a hundred new cards one day, a hundred the next, maybe a hundred the day after that, and then the fourth day comes around and there are 800 reviews waiting. Another sign is still being up at midnight doing cards when I normally go to bed at eleven, and not having a good time. And the sneakiest one is chasing a number somebody else set, like 3,000 words a month or ten hours a day. That’s a hundred words a day, and I don’t want to be a Debbie Downer, but I just don’t think that’s realistic.

So now I try to catch myself and ask whether I’m chasing some number or just free-flowing and enjoying Japanese the way I want to. If it’s midnight and I’m hating my reviews, I put the phone down and go to bed. Burnout isn’t only about how many cards you’re doing, either. If you look at my review heatmap from a couple of years ago, there’s a big empty stretch right in the middle, and that was an internship a few states away where I was alone, with no friends or anyone around for support, and I just crashed out. When the rest of your life gets lonely or stressful, go easy on yourself, shrink Japanese down to something small you still enjoy, and build back up once things settle.

Sleep

I’d say sleep is probably your best immersion tool. It recharges your brain, which is the very thing that’s trying to learn a new language, and it’s when the stuff you took in during the day actually gets stored. Staying up late to hit some quota like four or five hours of immersion was one of the things that helped me the least. So rest up, and rest properly, and don’t do what I do and average like three hours a night. It’s terrible.

Routines that survive bad days

When I was in school, the first hour after I got home was non-negotiable Japanese, at least my reviews and a bit of immersion, and if I was having fun it spilled over into the evening. Timeboxing helped too, like reading for fifty minutes and then getting up for ten. (It does not work on my study-with-me streams. I yap too much with chat.) Short and consistent beats long and rare, and the only person worth comparing yourself to is you from six months ago.

Spotting fake “fast fluency”

You’re going to see courses and videos promising fluency in three or six months, usually with a course behind them, or someone leaving out how many years of classes came first. You could pay someone to spoon-feed you and tuck you in so you could study every waking hour, and three months still wouldn’t be enough. Everything those courses sell comes down to something free: more time in, consistently. Tutors, exchanges and communities like Matt vs Japan’s can be worth paying for; shortcuts aren’t.

The worst of it right now is ads. I’ve seen two “I learned English in three months with this app” ads that were basically the same video, both AI-generated, starring people who don’t exist. Another one said I could be fluent in a month with five to ten minutes a day with an AI tutor. Do the math: that’s two and a half to five hours of practice, total. Look up how many unique words are in an anime you love on jpdb and ask yourself whether five hours covers that. When the thirty days are up and you still can’t watch your show, it’s really easy to decide that you’re bad at languages, and you’re not. They’re selling dopamine, not results. Real progress looks like understanding one more sentence than last time, or recognizing a word you mined three weeks ago.

Find your people

One of the most important things I learned was to find a community, a group of people to talk to about the shows you’re watching and the words you just picked up. It creates this camaraderie, study buddies who challenge each other along the way, and the advanced stage especially is a long, quiet stretch that’s a lot less lonely with people around. That’s why our Discord exists. There are hundreds of motivated learners at every level in there, so come hang out.

The advanced stretch

Near the top, you stop feeling your progress from one day to the next, and from what people tell me, it’s one of the longest stretches of the whole journey. The answer isn’t a new method. You just keep repeating the loop: find stuff to immerse in, make cards, review them, immerse some more, pick harder content when things get comfy, and do your hobbies in Japanese. So don’t stress. You’ve already come this far. Just keep pushing and come back to it every day, and you will make it, inevitably. (I said that last part in a video I filmed outside in sub-zero weather, shivering, and I meant it.)
