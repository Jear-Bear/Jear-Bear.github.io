Japanese has three writing systems working together: hiragana (the round, grammatical one), katakana (the angular one for foreign words and emphasis), and kanji (the Chinese characters). Kanji gets its own chapter. Today is just the first two.

Good news: kana is the easiest part of Japanese. It’s about 46 basic characters each, plus a few marks. Most people get them down in a few days to a week.

Learn kana in days, not months

  • Hiragana first, then katakana. You’ll see hiragana on literally every line of Japanese, so it sticks fast.
  • Use mnemonics. き looks like a key, ぬ is a noodle with a loop. Silly works.
  • Drill in short bursts. Ten minutes a few times a day beats one miserable two-hour session.
  • “Down” means you can read them, not that you never hesitate. Once you can read kana slowly, move on. Your flashcards and everything you read from now on will keep drilling them for free.
  • Katakana will be a weak spot for a while. It shows up less, so it stays slow longer. Totally normal. Reading menus, games and loanword-heavy stuff fixes it.

Is it bad to learn words before you know kana?

No. If you learn that sakana means fish from romaji, then later see さかな in hiragana, your brain goes “oh, sakana, fish!” and you’ve lost nothing. Lots of people do this and it’s fine.

The thing to avoid is staying on romaji. As soon as you can read kana, switch everything (flashcards, notes, the way you type) to kana. Romaji is a crutch that makes reading actual Japanese slower forever if you lean on it.

Get a Japanese keyboard on everything

You’ll need to type Japanese to look up words, search YouTube, take notes and eventually text people. Every device has a free Japanese input method (IME) built in:

  • Windows: Settings → Time & language → Language & region → Add a language → Japanese. Switch with Alt + ` or Win + Space.
  • Mac: System Settings → Keyboard → Input Sources → add Japanese – Romaji. Switch with Ctrl + Space.
  • iPhone / Android: add the Japanese keyboard in settings. You can type romaji on the normal QWERTY layout, or try the flick (12-key) layout, where you tap a kana and swipe for its row. It secretly drills kana into your thumbs.

How it works: you type romaji (nihongo), it shows にほんご, you press Space to convert to 日本語, and Enter to confirm. Typing ん sometimes needs nn. Small っ comes from doubling the next consonant (kitte → きって).

What do Japanese people actually type with?

Mostly romaji, same as you. Back in 1990, more people in Japan used kana input (a layout where every key is a kana) than romaji. Now it’s flipped hard: in recent surveys around 94% type in romaji and only about 5% use kana input. That’s roughly 1 person in 20.

The phone one surprised me even more. You’ll hear that “every Japanese person uses flick input.” In a DOCOMO survey of about 6,000 people, fewer than half of every age group used flick. The highest was women aged 15 to 24, at 47%. So don’t feel like a fake for typing romaji on your phone. I use romaji on my PC and my phone.

Flick is still worth a try if it sounds fun. When I started I could do about 8 characters in 20 seconds. With practice I got to 21 characters in 21 seconds, and most people can get comfortable in a month or two.

Do you need to write by hand?

Short version: it depends on your goals. Thanks to the IME, you can read, type, text and work in Japanese without ever handwriting a kanji. I can write kana and only about 50 kanji from memory, but I can read around 2,600. That’s a pretty normal gap for immersion learners.

That said, if you enjoy it, think it’s cool, or want to really break kanji down into their parts, go for it. My friend DASmallWorlds (JLPT N1, studying for the Kanji Kentei level 1) makes a great case that writing helps you tell apart look-alikes like 待 and 持. Writing kana by hand at least once is worth it, just to make the shapes stick.

要TL;DR

  • Kana: a few days to a week. Read slowly = good enough to move on.
  • Romaji early is fine. Staying on romaji is not.
  • Install a Japanese keyboard on your phone and computer today.
  • Handwriting: optional, depending on your goals (and fun if you like it).
