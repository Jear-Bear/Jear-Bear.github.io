You don’t need to memorize harder. You just need a system that remembers for you.

— me

I used to think the more words I memorized in a day, the faster I’d get good. So I crammed. Before my JLPT N2, I memorized 300 words the night before. Did it help the next day? Yes. Did I remember them the next week? Not a chance. That’s short-term memory. An SRS is how you turn the words you meet into long-term memory without burning out.

How many new cards a day?

Here’s the trap: every new card you add today turns into reviews for months. Ten new cards a day isn’t ten cards a day. Play with it:

話Story time

When I first started with Anki, I did 5 new cards a day and thought, “This is way too easy.” So I bumped it to 50. A few weeks later I had hundreds of reviews every morning, started dreading the app, burned out, and didn’t touch Japanese for three months. Going fast cost me way more time than going slow would have.

What I recommend:

  • Start at 5 a day for a week or even a month.
  • Go up slowly, 5 at a time, only when your daily reviews feel comfortable.
  • Most people land around 10–20. Right now I do 10 new words plus 5 new grammar cards a day while I prep for the N1.
  • Aim for 80–90% retention. If you’re passing fewer than about 75% of your reviews, you’re adding too much. Slow down for a week.
  • Watch for the signs. Staying up past your bedtime doing reviews you’re not enjoying? Close the app and go to sleep. Seriously.

Which words are worth a card?

Two quick checks before you hit the mine button:

  1. Is the sentence i+1? “i+1” means you know every word in the sentence except one. All the other words become context that tells you what the new one means. If a sentence has five words you don’t know, the card will be a slog. Skip it and find a better one; there are a million more.
  2. Is the word common? Check the frequency number in your popup. The average native speaker knows around 30,000 words; a word ranked #27,000 isn’t going to give you much bang for your buck yet.

My rough rule:

  • Beginner: mine words inside the top 10,000.
  • Intermediate: most of what I mined sat between 10,000 and 20,000. Less frequent, still really useful in everyday conversation.
  • Advanced: whatever keeps showing up in your content. If you play a lot of JRPGs, fantasy words will be way more common for you than the list says.

And if you think a word will stick easily and you get the meaning, there’s no real harm in adding it. These are rules of thumb, not laws.

Don’t become an Anki drone

I used to be an Anki drone. Here’s what that looked like:

  • I’d see a card, think “oh, I know this word,” and hit Good without actually reading the sentence. Hundreds of reviews done, almost nothing learned.
  • I’d recognize words in the wild because I had cards for them, but still not understand the sentence. My only picture of each word came from one example sentence, and real Japanese kept using it in ways that didn’t fit.

Some words click the first time you see them. Others need five or ten different sentences before you get the vibe. Take 抱く: read daku, it means physically holding something; read idaku, it means harboring a feeling. With one card for one of them, you’ll overgeneralize and sound weird. Seeing a word fifteen times in fifteen different shows beats repping the same card fifteen times.

Every time you see a word in the wild, your brain makes a guess at what it means, and the context either confirms it or corrects it. You see 食べる in a sentence about sushi: confirmed, “to eat.” Then you see 食べていく and wonder why this guy is eating money, look it up, and learn it also means “make a living.” Your mental model just got a little sharper. That never happens inside a flashcard.

SRS has its place, but it shouldn’t be at the forefront of your language journey.

— me, reacting to a “flashcards beat immersion” video

Flashcards vs immersion: how much of each?

It depends on your level:

  • Beginner: more flashcards. You’re building the base vocabulary that makes immersion possible.
  • Intermediate: still a good chunk, since you’re finding tons of unknown words. But immersion should already be most of your time.
  • Advanced: mostly immersion. You run into fewer unknowns, so you need more hours of content to find them.

Right now, less than 10% of my Japanese time goes to flashcards. Some days after work I’ll do 20 minutes of reviews and then just watch whatever I want. That’s not laziness; that is the method. I started Japanese because I found it beautiful, not to swipe cards like a zombie.

Why immersion does the heavy lifting

Something that changed how I think about this came from the linguist Dr. Jeff McQuillan: vocabulary is acquired incidentally and incrementally.

  • Incidentally means you pick words up while you’re busy following a story, not while you’re sitting there thinking “okay, time to learn vocab.” Opening your vocab deck is pretty much the opposite of that.
  • Incrementally means you get a bit more of a word each time you meet it. Studies by a researcher named William Nagy found it’s roughly 10–15% more per encounter. First you just know it’s a verb, or that it’s something bad. A few contexts later you just know it, and you couldn’t tell anyone where you learned it.

And here’s the kicker: the words you really need show up more often, by definition. Reading is its own SRS. In Anki, a rare word and a common word come up just as often. In real content, the common ones keep coming back and the rare ones fade. That’s why I keep easing off the flashcards and leaning into reading and watching more.

It also means your card count undersells you. Having 8,000 cards doesn’t mean you know 8,000 words. You probably know more, because plenty of words stick just from running into them again and again.

Backlogs and the “mine everything” question

At one point I was mining every unknown word I saw, and ended up with a 4,600-card backlog. Every word in there was useful. They were my babies. I had separation anxiety. I deleted the whole thing anyway, and it felt so much better.

If something’s rare or obscure, either skip it or mark it as known so it stops bugging you. It’ll come back later if it matters.

友Honorable mention: Wyatt (Furretar)

My friend Wyatt mined every unknown word he ever came across, and he’s just cracked. I’m not sure I’d recommend it to most people (see: my backlog), but it’s proof that there’s more than one way up the mountain if you have the discipline for it.

Card tricks that help

  • Picture cards for nouns. A picture of a kid running late sticks better than the English “I’ve got to go.”
  • Listening cards: audio on the front, word and definition on the back, if listening is your weak spot.
  • Grammar cards: mine a sentence where the grammar point is the one unknown. Same i+1 rule.
  • Personal memories stick. I’ll never forget おなら (fart) because of the anime scene I mined it from. Weird example, I know. Bear with me. Context is everything.
How to ACTUALLY Memorize Japanese Vocab

要TL;DR

  • Start at 5 new cards a day. Most people settle around 10–20. Never 50.
  • Mine i+1 sentences with common words (top 10k as a beginner).
  • Actually read the sentence on every card.
  • Flashcards fill gaps; immersion builds the real understanding.
  • Delete a backlog that’s stressing you out. It’ll be fine.
