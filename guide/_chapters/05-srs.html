I was always under the impression that the more words I memorized in a day, the faster I’d get good. And man, was I wrong. Before my JLPT N2 I may or may not have crammed 300 words the night before the exam. Did it help the next day? Yes. Did I remember any of them the next week? No. That’s short-term memory. You don’t need to memorize harder, you just need a system that remembers for you, and that’s what an SRS is.

How many new cards a day?

The trap is that every new card you add today turns into reviews for months. You can play with that here:

When I first started with Anki I did five new cards a day and thought, “this is way too easy,” so I bumped it up to fifty. A few weeks later I had hundreds of reviews every morning, started dreading the app, burned out, and didn’t touch Japanese for three months. In the end, going fast cost me way more time than going slow would have.

So what I recommend is starting at five a day for a week, or even a month, and only going up a few at a time when your daily reviews feel comfortable. Most people end up somewhere around ten to twenty. Right now I’m doing ten new words and five new grammar cards a day while I prep for the N1. Aim to get somewhere around 80 to 90% of your reviews right; if you’re down below about 75%, you’re adding too much, so slow down for a week. And if you catch yourself up past your bedtime doing reviews you’re not enjoying, that’s a sign too. Put the phone down and go to bed.

Which words are worth a card?

When you mine from stuff you’re watching or reading, the best sentences are what people call i+1: you know every word except one. Say you’re learning English and you hear “this book is interesting,” and the only word you don’t know is “book.” Everything else in the sentence becomes context for the new word, especially if you heard it from your favorite YouTuber in a scene you remember. If a sentence has five words you don’t know, the card is going to be a slog, so skip it. There are a million more.

The other thing to check is how common the word is. When you’re starting out, most people recommend sticking to the top 10,000 or so on the frequency number in your popup, and at the intermediate stage most of what I mined sat between 10,000 and 20,000, still really useful in everyday conversation. These are rules of thumb, though; if a word seems like it’ll stick, there’s no real harm in adding it.

Don’t become an Anki drone

You may not know this about me, but I used to be an Anki drone. I’d see a card, think “oh, I know this one,” and hit Good without actually reading the sentence or asking myself what the word was doing in it. Hundreds of reviews done, not much learned. The other problem is that when you mine a word and only ever review it in that one sentence, you end up recognizing it in the wild without really understanding it, because the way it’s being used doesn’t line up with the one picture of it you have in your head.

Some words you get the first time. Others you need to see in a bunch of different sentences before you get the vibe. 抱く read as daku means to physically hold something, but read as idaku it means to harbor a feeling, so if you only had a card for the first one, a sentence about someone harboring a grudge reads like she’s hugging it. Every time you see a word in a new context, your brain makes a guess and the context confirms or corrects it. You see 食べる in a sentence about sushi and think “yep, to eat.” Then you see it in a sentence where some guy is apparently eating money, look it up, and find out it can also mean making a living. That kind of correction basically never happens inside a flashcard.

Why immersion does most of the work

Something that changed how I think about this came from a video by the linguist Dr. Jeff McQuillan. His point is that vocabulary is acquired incidentally and incrementally. Incidentally means you pick words up while you’re busy following what’s going on, not while you’re sitting there going “okay, time to learn vocab,” which is pretty much exactly what opening a vocab deck is. Incrementally means you pick up a little more of a word each time you see it; there were studies by a researcher named William Nagy that put it at around 10 to 15% more per encounter. Eventually you just know the word, and you couldn’t tell anyone when you learned it. The words you really need show up more often, by definition, so in a way reading is its own SRS. In Anki, a rare word and a common word come up just as often. In real content, the common ones keep coming back.

That’s why I keep easing off the gas on flashcards, and these days less than 10% of my Japanese time goes to them. As a beginner it should be a lot more, since you’re building the base that makes immersion possible at all, but the further you get, the more of your time should be immersion. It also means your card count undersells you: 8,000 cards doesn’t mean you know 8,000 words, you probably know more. I started Japanese because I found it beautiful, not to swipe cards like a zombie.

Backlogs, and mining everything

At one point I was mining every unknown word I came across, and ended up with a backlog of 4,600 cards. Every word in there was useful. They were my babies. I had separation anxiety. I deleted the whole thing anyway, and it felt so much better. If something’s rare or obscure, either skip it or just mark it as known so it stops bugging you; if it matters, it’ll come back. (That said, my friend Wyatt, Furretar on YouTube, mined every single unknown word he ever saw, and he’s just cracked. So there’s more than one way up the mountain if you have the discipline for it. I just don’t think it’s for most people.)

A couple of card tricks that have helped me: nouns can have really long, hard-to-pin-down definitions, so a picture on the card helps a lot. And the cards that stick best are the ones with a memory attached. I’ll never forget おなら (fart) because of the anime scene I mined it from. Weird example, I know. Bear with me. But that’s context doing its job.
