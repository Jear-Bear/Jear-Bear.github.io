A while back someone told me I just needed to read more, that it was the true path to getting better at Japanese. So of course I went to the library, grabbed the first Japanese book I could find, opened it up and struggled. No matter how many words I looked up, I was completely lost. Years later, reading is what I spend most of my Japanese time on, at least three hours a day this year, with a goal of 1,000 hours and 30 books. This chapter is how I got from that library book to here.

Why reading matters so much

When I passed the N2 on my third try, my listening basically carried me, while reading, vocab and grammar were way behind, because I’d spent a year mostly watching stuff. Reading is just way more information-dense than listening. An hour of a light novel puts a lot more words in front of you than an hour of anime, and you can slow down for the hard parts. Once I made reading my main thing, my known words started shooting up, and weirdly, speaking got easier too, because more words in your head means more words that come out.

What to read at each level

Beginner

At the start, the goal is confidence and consistency. Graded readers are books written for learners in levels, and the Tadoku NPO has a bunch of free ones. NHK News Web Easy rewrites real news in simpler Japanese for kids and foreigners, and it’s a great bridge if you’re around N4 to N3 (if you’ve done Genki 1 and 2, you’d probably be fine with it). And manga with furigana, especially Yotsuba&!, which is the classic first manga. LivingJapanese has a free reading pack for the first volume with every word and sentence in an Anki deck, which makes it a lot more manageable.

Intermediate

A decent sign you’re here is hitting only one or two unknown words per NHK Easy article. This is where I started reading about my hobbies, like switching my recipes and workout plans to Japanese. Other than that: lots of slice-of-life manga, then shōnen (Jujutsu Kaisen and Dandadan are surprisingly approachable), light novels like また、同じ夢を見ていた and 君の膵臓をたべたい by Sumino Yoru or コンビニ人間, and visual novels, which are reading but feel like playing a game, so my stamina lasts way longer with them than with a plain book.

Advanced

Here it’s mostly stamina and taste. I moved from NHK Easy to the regular NHK site, and binge-reading one topic works really well: I read a whole run of articles about bear attacks in Hokkaido and came out knowing a bunch of nature and ecosystem words. I’m a murder-mystery nerd, so Ayatsuji Yukito’s 館 series and a Japanese translation of Murder on the Orient Express are on my list, and someday the classics like Natsume Sōseki’s 坊っちゃん, which took me thirty minutes on stream to get through half of the first page. Most of my reading this year has been 本好きの下剋上 (Ascendance of a Bookworm), thirty-plus volumes, a bit slow-paced and really great, and next I want 86 to branch out into military vocab, since I’ve gotten pretty comfortable with isekai and fantasy words.

Your Japanese bookshelf / the stack of Bookworm volumes

Most of my books are EPUBs that I read in the ッツ reader with the audiobook synced up, so the text highlights as it’s read aloud. It makes it easier to keep pace and read faster, but I still do plenty of raw reading so I don’t lean on it too much.

Making reading feel doable

You ever ask someone for reading advice and all you get is “just read more”? Wow, revolutionary. It’s true, but it’s not much help when you’re looking up every other word and you’re burnt out by page two. A few things made it click for me. The first one I got from MoeWay: pay attention to the nouns. In English nouns jump out at you, but in Japanese they blend into the rest of the sentence, and once I started spotting the people, places and things first, my brain kind of reorganized the sentence around them. It also helps to break a paragraph into chunks and take it one phrase or sentence at a time, like a puzzle, instead of staring at the whole wall of text. Kanji stop being scary once you see them as a few familiar parts (more on that in the next chapter), and furigana is a great crutch at the start as long as you wean yourself off it little by little.

Something I don’t see recommended much is reading a summary in your own language first. Knowing the plot and who’s who gives you a scaffold to hang new words on, and it’s not cheating, it’s setting the stage. Repetition does a lot of quiet work too. 〜なければならない, 〜なくてはいけない and 〜ないといけない all mean “have to,” and after enough reading your eyes just skim over them the same way you skim “I gotta get outta here” in English. And last, keep it fun. The moment reading becomes a chore you’ll stop doing it, so read stuff you want to read, not stuff you think you should.

7 Tricks That Instantly Boosted My Japanese Reading

My first novel

The first full novel I finished was また、同じ夢を見ていた. I only start a book once I know about 98% of its words on jpdb (not a magic number, 98 just sounded cool), because at that point most lookups are names and obscure words. For harder books I raise that number first, by watching the anime or movie adaptation and going through the vocab list. While reading, when a line doesn’t click, I close my eyes and picture what the Japanese describes instead of translating it. And when I finished, I read a Japanese reader’s blog post about the ending to see if my interpretation matched theirs. It mostly did, although I completely missed a joke about one character’s name.

精読, 速読, 多読

Scroll r/LearnJapanese long enough and every reading method turns into an argument, but I think you cycle through all three. 精読 (seidoku), intensive reading, is slow and analytical: look up the grammar, parse the sentence, mine. It builds accuracy, and I used to do it for two hours a day, which I could feel working and could also feel burning me out. 速読 (sokudoku), reading at a set pace so you react instead of translate, helped my perfectionism a lot. And 多読 (tadoku), extensive reading with minimal lookups, works like a natural SRS because you keep seeing the same words, as long as you understand most of what you’re reading.

Stopping all the time breaks your flow, but ignoring confusion stops you from growing. So my balance is to do intensive reading on stuff I can mostly understand, and go with the flow, with fewer lookups, on stuff that’s a bit above me. If you comprehend enough of something, you can infer most of the words without looking them up anyway.

My manga picks, by level

I only list manga I’ve actually read, so no Death Note or One Piece yet. I’m working on it.

  • Yotsuba&!よつばと！

    The gateway drug. Kid protagonist, simple speech, everyday words, full furigana.

  • Komi Can’t Communicate古見さんは、コミュ症です。

    Absolute cinema. Furigana throughout, and the cast keeps growing, so you get tons of speech styles.

  • Mieruko-chan見える子ちゃん

    Slice of life with horror. Big visual panels break up the text. I read it around N3 and it didn’t feel like a kids’ manga.

  • Karada Sagashiカラダ探し

    Gory, fast, modern high-school setting. The time-loop plot is basically free SRS.

  • The Dangers in My Heart僕の心のヤバイやつ

    Slow-burn romcom in a middle school, full of the protagonist’s inner monologue.

  • You and I Are Polar Opposites正反対な君と僕

    One of my favorites. Natural romance dialogue and a lot of modern slang about hair, makeup and nails.

  • Dandadanダンダダン

    Every human on earth should read it. Yokai, aliens, urban legends; fast and funny enough to carry you through the hard bits.

  • Boukyaku Battery忘却バッテリー

    Baseball with an amnesia plot, so they re-explain the basics. Great even if you don’t like sports.

  • Golden Boyゴールデンボーイ

    Cursed. Older readers only. The humor hits harder in Japanese. I’m not going to cope about this one.

  • Hunter × HunterHUNTER×HUNTER

    Famous for gargantuan text walls, and invented terms like 念 that mean something different from real life. I’ve watched the anime five times.

  • Bakiグラップラー刃牙

    If you’ve ever wanted testosterone in manga form. As a gym guy, this does wonders for me.

  • JoJo’s Part 3: Stardust Crusadersジョジョの奇妙な冒険

    Lots of speech styles across the main cast, and lots of new words explaining Stand powers.

  • Hajime no Ippoはじめの一歩

    I quit it the first time and came back when I was ready. Training and boxing vocab, and the old coaches talk differently. Training, fighting, getting stronger… it’s just like AJATT.

When reading starts to feel easy

This year there have been sessions where I read for thirty minutes, sometimes a couple of hours, without thinking in English at all, and it almost feels like reading in English. It’s a really good feeling, but I think it’s also a sign that I need to start choosing harder stuff, because I don’t want to get in the habit of staying comfortable. The harder material is where the unknown words are, and that’s where I learn the most. And on the flip side, if you genuinely want to read something, don’t let the difficulty scare you off. Take it bit by bit, be consistent, and close the book when you’re tired. Even native speakers do that.
