I spent so many hours on conjugation tables. Slamming my head against the wall until enlightenment came to me is exactly how I passed the JLPT. Not. Here’s what actually worked.

The biggest grammar mistake

A lot of people think: if I memorize enough vocab and enough grammar points, I’ll be able to understand and build sentences. It works at first. Then you hit intermediate, and the grammar starts having overlapping meanings and nuances that textbooks just don’t capture.

Example: 渡る and 越える both translate to “cross.” So “cross the bridge” could be 橋を越える, right? Except 越える carries an image of getting over an obstacle. 橋を越える sounds like the bridge is in your way and you’re climbing over it. You want 橋を渡る. No flashcard tells you that. Hearing both used a hundred times does.

And in a real conversation there’s no time to stop and assemble the grammatically optimal sentence. Awkward silence is awkward. If you’ve ever seen those Death Note clips with the inner monologue cut out, you know what I mean.

Grammar is mostly just more words

友SevenStop’s take

The vast majority of Japanese grammar is just more words. If you learn words in context and see how they’re used, you naturally pick up conjugations, sentence structure, particles, all of it.

So here’s the approach:

  1. Skim a grammar guide once (Tae Kim or Sakubi, chapter 3). Get the rough map.
  2. Immerse. When something unfamiliar shows up (〜ようになる, 〜わけ, 〜ものの), look it up right then.
  3. Mine it. Grammar goes on flashcards just like words: an i+1 sentence where the grammar is the one new thing. Right now I add 5 grammar cards a day on top of my 10 words while I prep for the N1.
  4. See it in other places. Search the grammar point on Immersion Kit and watch it used in five different anime. Each one adds to your feel for it.

The more you see a pattern, the more it just clicks without you realizing. It’s how you learned grammar in your native language.

Do I ever study grammar explicitly?

Sometimes, yes. Transitive vs intransitive verbs (動く vs 動かす) broke my brain in high school, so I studied the table in Genki 2 once, explicitly. Then immersion did the rest. Now I can usually guess both versions of a new verb. And if you need to speak soon, say you’re going to Japan in three months, it’s fine to drill the conjugations you’ll need for that trip. Long term, they come to you.

Most of the time, you don’t say “I”

Textbooks teach 私 on day one, so learners put it in every sentence. But Japanese leaves out whatever is obvious from context. If you say 昨日ラーメンを食べた, it’s already clear who ate. Sticking 私は on the front of every line is one of the fastest ways to sound like a textbook.

When you do need one, the word you choose says something about you:

  • 私 (watashi): the safe, polite default, and pretty much the default for women in any setting.
  • 僕 (boku): mostly guys; gentle, a bit casual.
  • 俺 (ore): casual and confident, for close friends. Use it with your boss or a stranger and you sound rude or like you want to fight.
  • あたし, うち, 自分, わし, わたくし: cute and casual, Kansai, sporty or military, old man, ultra formal.
  • 拙者, 吾輩, 俺様, 朕: samurai dramas, Natsume Sōseki’s cat, anime villains, and literally only the emperor. Fun to recognize, not to use.

You’ll pick up which characters use which just from watching. That’s the point: grammar and nuance come from seeing words used by real people.

15 Ways to Say “I” in Japanese (and When to Use Them)

Stop translating in your head

Here’s a loop a lot of learners get stuck in: hear Japanese → translate to English → think of a reply in English → translate it back → say it. By the time you answer, the moment has passed.

Japanese doesn’t map one-to-one to English. Every time you route through English, you’re training your brain to process English first. My favorite example of why that’s a dead end:

Is かける “to hang”? “To wear”? “To call”? It’s all of those, and more. At some point English just can’t keep up, and that’s your sign to start learning Japanese in Japanese.

Think in pictures

The trick that helped me most: when I hear a sentence, I close my eyes and picture the scene. No English in the middle. Japanese → image, not Japanese → English → image.

Small phrases work the same way. When I hear 行かなきゃ, I don’t think “verb stem, negative, conditional, contraction, means I have to go.” I just see someone grabbing their bag and rushing out. That’s the goal for everything.

Going monolingual

For your first 2,000–3,000 words, leaning on English is totally fine. It’s the fastest way to get that first layer in. After that, start switching your dictionary to Japanese definitions.

  • When: around 3,000 known words. By then you can understand most simple Japanese definitions, with a lookup here and there.
  • How: add Japanese–Japanese (monolingual) dictionaries to Yomitan and put them first. TheMoeWay has a good collection. Keep an English one lower in the list as a backup.
  • Worried it’ll take too long? Reading dictionary definitions is immersion. You’re reading Japanese explained in Japanese. That counts.
  • Don’t get a definition? Mine the words in it, or look up a few example sentences instead.

Every definition in Japanese connects a new word to words you already know. It’s like building a spiderweb with no English in the middle. That’s how you build a Japanese brain.

Thinking in Japanese

Start small. Not full sentences, just little thoughts during your day:

  • You’re hungry: お腹すいた…
  • You can’t find your keys in the morning rush: 鍵どこだっけ？
  • The weather’s nice: いい天気だな

Then narrate your day. Talk to your cat, your dog, your plants. Pretend you’re a Japanese YouTuber explaining how to make onigiri. It feels silly. Do it anyway. Your inner voice slowly starts switching languages, and the less you translate, the faster you react in real conversations. (This is the safe, talk-to-yourself kind of output. Talking to actual people comes in the next chapter.)

話Story time: the chopstick incident

At a konbini, the clerk asked if I needed chopsticks: お箸いりますか？ (いる, to need). My brain heard 入れる (to put in) and decided the clerk was offering to insert the chopsticks… inside of me. I froze. That’s what translating word-by-word under pressure gets you. Now I just hear “chopsticks?” and say お願いします.
