Before we get into tools and decks, I want to talk about the thing that decides whether you actually stick with this, which is why you’re doing it in the first place. Everyone starts out excited. The novelty carries you through kana and your first few hundred words, and then somewhere in the intermediate stage progress gets slow and you stop being able to see it. That’s where most people quit, and it’s where I quit, more than once.

Mine

I started Japanese for anime. I stayed because of a host family in Morioka.

  1. High school, TexasI signed up for Japanese on a whim, did Genki 1 and 2, and placed in the top three at a competition called the Japan Bowl, which got me a trip to Japan.
  2. My first homestay, MoriokaUp in Tohoku, out in the countryside. My host family didn’t speak English and I didn’t really speak Japanese. They had all these fun places planned, and all I could say over and over was “this is a pen” and “where’s the toilet.” There was so much we both wanted to say and just couldn’t, and it was really frustrating.
  3. CollegeI came back and hit the books, then the videos, then the novels. I tried AJATT for the first time, did twelve hours a day, and burned out. I took Tobira in a college class and worked at a Japanese grocery store, where I got to actually use Japanese with my coworkers.
  4. Two years laterI saved up and flew back to see that same family on my own, outside of any program. I wasn’t fluent, but I could do everyday conversation, and we actually talked. I won’t forget the smiles on their faces, or how it felt on mine.
  5. Since thenMore trips, more friends. Renting a car and driving around Hokkaido, chatting with strangers, getting drinks with salarymen at bars, getting stopped for a street interview. I haven’t quit since that second visit.

Why a reason beats motivation

Motivation comes and goes. A reason is something you can go back to when it’s gone. Funnily enough, my problem as a beginner wasn’t a lack of motivation, it was too much of it. I’d sit down and go for five or six hours straight, then burn out and not touch Japanese for weeks. Even now, when I can enjoy most native content, I couldn’t imagine doing that every day. If you’re overflowing with excitement today, that’s awesome, but spend some of it and save the rest for tomorrow.

What actually kept me going was having real people at the end of it, stuff I genuinely wanted to understand, and other people learning alongside me.

Pin yours

Write down why you’re learning Japanese, and be specific about it. “To be fluent” is a goal, not a reason. “To understand my favorite streamer without subtitles,” “to talk to my partner’s grandma,” or “to read the manga that never got translated” are reasons.

Whatever you write shows up on the guide’s front page every time you come back. On a bad day, read it, and then do ten minutes of something you enjoy in Japanese. That still counts.

About the word “fluent”

People throw “fluent” around like it’s a finish line, and it really isn’t. My parents, who haven’t studied Japanese, hear me talk and think I’m basically native. A Japanese person would say I’m a foreigner with pretty good Japanese. By my own unrealistic, perfectionist standards I’m terrible at it. You just can’t win. So I’d rather your goal be the thing your reason is about: watching the show, talking to the family, reading the book. You get to start doing those things way earlier than you’d think, badly at first, and doing them badly is honestly a lot of fun. If you want a working definition, when I say fluent in this guide I mean roughly CEFR B2, where you can follow most native content and hold your own in a normal conversation.
