Hey, I’m Jared. About eight years ago I got into Japanese because I wanted to be the guy who could watch anime without subtitles. I thought that would be so awesome. I was in high school, and somehow my school in Texas had Japanese classes, so I signed up on a whim and started with Genki 1 and 2.

After both books I felt like I was on top of the world and basically fluent. Then I went to Japan, stayed with a host family, and could barely scrape by on surface-level daily Japanese. I was crushed. That trip is the reason I’m still doing this, and I’ll tell that story in the next chapter.

What followed was about six years of doing it the “normal” way: textbooks, a college course, Tobira, vocab lists, and failing the JLPT N2 twice. Somewhere in there I tried immersion, did it in a really unhealthy way, and burned out. Around two years ago I started over with AJATT and did it properly, and that’s what finally got me reading novels and actually talking to my host family. This guide is everything I picked up along the way, dumb mistakes included.

What even is AJATT?

AJATT stands for All Japanese All The Time (you’ll also hear MIA, the Mass Immersion Approach). Instead of studying about Japanese, you spend as much of your life as you can in it: anime, YouTube, games, manga, novels, podcasts, whatever you’re into. The internet makes that ridiculously easy now, and you get to tailor it to exactly what you like and your level.

That said, “just immerse, bro” isn’t the whole story. If getting fluent was as simple as watching a bunch of anime, every weeb on Earth would be cracked at Japanese, myself included. Immersion works when you pair it with a couple of simple tools and pick stuff that’s actually at your level, and that’s most of what this guide is about.

The loop

My friend Hypothetical Tiger sums up the whole method better than I can: immerse, make cards from your immersion, rep your cards, and repeat until you’re fluent. Everything else in this guide is a detail of one of those four steps.

1 · Immerse 浸

Watch, read, play and listen to Japanese stuff you genuinely enjoy, at a level where you understand most of it.

2 · Mine 掘

When a sentence has just one word you don’t know, save it as a flashcard. With the right tools it’s a couple of clicks.

3 · Review 復

Your flashcard app shows you each card right before you’d forget it. Ten or twenty minutes a day.

4 · Repeat 又

You understand more, so harder stuff opens up, so you find new words, and around it goes.

Underneath the loop there’s one rule I keep coming back to, which is that you get better at the thing you actually do. If you want to read better, read. If you want to understand anime, watch anime. If you want to speak, at some point you’re going to have to speak. I used to be a swimmer, and you know how I got faster at swimming? I went to swim practice.

It’s not a race

If you only take one thing from this guide, I want it to be this, because it took me the longest to learn. You’re going to see videos promising fluency in three months, and people who got “fluent in eight months,” and you’re going to wonder why you’re years in and still struggling. I’ve been there. Over my eight years I probably quit about four or five times, and almost every time it was because of that grindset mindset. The intermediate stage is where it got me the most.

Some quick math that helped me: the average human lifespan is around 700,000 hours, and it takes something like 2,200 hours for an English speaker to get comfortable in Japanese. So what’s the rush? I could speed to my plans across a city I’ve never seen, or take my time and enjoy the scenery, and Japanese is the scenery. I’m doing this for the love of the game. I also work a full-time cybersecurity job, so I don’t have ten hours a day like some of the students getting fluent in a year, and once I accepted that it would take me longer, I stopped quitting.

The way I like to picture it, every word and kanji you learn is one more stone on your own Japanese mountain. Some days you add a lot, some days none, but it only ever goes up. (The stone at the top is 凪, nagi, a dead calm at sea. It’s my favorite kanji.)

How to use this guide

If you’re a total beginner, go in order. If you’re already immersing, jump to whatever’s bugging you; every chapter stands on its own, and your progress saves in your browser. Everything here is what I actually do, so take what works for you and ignore the rest.
