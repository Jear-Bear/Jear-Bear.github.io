    Staying in it

    続けるコツ

    Burnout, sleep, routines, comparison, scams and community. The unglamorous stuff that decides whether you’re still doing this in three years.

    10 min readEveryone✓ Read

    続

    I’ve quit Japanese four or five times. The longest break was about four months, and every single day of it, half of me was going “dude, you have to study, you’re getting worse,” and the other half was going “I hate Japanese, I’m so tired of this.” It was miserable. Everything in this chapter is what I wish I’d known before each of those quits.

    Know your burnout signs

    Burnout doesn’t show up out of nowhere. For me there are always warning signs:

    • Splurges. 100 new cards on Monday, 100 on Tuesday, 100 on Wednesday… and on Thursday, 800 reviews waiting.
    • Doing reviews past my bedtime and not enjoying any of it.
    • Picking content because it’s “optimal” instead of because I want to watch it.
    • Chasing a number someone else set: 3,000 words a month, 10 hours a day.

    Now when I notice the signs, I catch myself. Am I chasing a number, or am I free-flowing and enjoying Japanese the way I want to? If I’m up at midnight doing cards and hating it, I put the phone down and go to bed.

    話Story time: the gap in my calendar

    If you look at my Anki heatmap from that year, there’s a big empty stretch right in the middle. That was an internship a few states away. I was alone, with no friends or anyone around for support, and I crashed out. Burnout isn’t only about how many cards you do; it hits hardest when the rest of your life gets lonely or stressful. When that happens, go easy on yourself. Shrink Japanese down to something tiny you’ll still enjoy, and build back up once life settles.

    Sleep is your best immersion tool

    Seriously. Your brain sorts and stores what you learned during the day while you sleep. That’s when short-term memory becomes long-term memory. Skipping sleep to hit an immersion quota actively undoes the work. (Do as I say, not as I do. At one point I was averaging three hours a night. Terrible. Don’t.)

    Routines that survive bad days

    • One non-negotiable hour. When I was in school, I got home around 5 and had about four free hours. The first hour was non-negotiable Japanese: at least my reviews and a bit of immersion. If I was having fun, it leaked into the rest of the evening. Even on low-motivation days, I got my reps in.
    • Timeboxing. Read for 50 minutes, get up for 10, walk around, grab a drink. (This does not work on my livestreams. I yap too much with chat.)
    • Short and consistent beats long and rare. One hour a day for a year wins against a twelve-hour weekend that ends in a three-month break.
    • Track it, lightly. I use Toggl. Watching the hours pile up is motivating; obsessing over them isn’t.

    Don’t compare yourself to anyone

    The intermediate stage is where I burned out the most, and the reason was always comparison. I’d see YouTubers who got fluent in eight months while I was four years in and questioning my existence. But I don’t know what goes on in their lives. Maybe they’re students with all day to study. Maybe they live with their parents. I work a full-time job. It was going to take me longer, and once I accepted that, I stopped quitting.

    You can only compare you to yourself. Look back at what you understood six months ago. That’s the only scoreboard that matters.

    Spotting fake “fast fluency”

    You’ll see courses and videos promising “fluent in 3 months” or “6 months.” Usually there’s a course behind it, or someone isn’t being fully honest about their study history (how many hours a day, how many years of classes before the “6 months” started). You could pay someone to spoon-feed you and tuck you in so you could study every waking hour, and 3 months still wouldn’t be enough to get remotely fluent.

    Everything those courses sell boils down to one sentence you can have for free: more time in, consistently, equals better. That’s it. That’s the secret. Some paid things are genuinely good, like tutors, coaching, or a community like Matt vs Japan’s. But be suspicious of anyone selling a shortcut.

    The worst of it right now is ads. I’ve seen two “I learned English in three months with this app” ads that were basically the same video, both AI-generated, with people who don’t exist. Another said I could be fluent in a month at 5–10 minutes a day with an AI tutor. Do the math: that’s two and a half to five hours of practice. Look up how many unique words are in an anime you love on jpdb and ask yourself if five hours covers that.

    Those ads also quietly redefine “fluent” as ordering a sandwich or chatting about the weather. Having any conversation in Japanese is awesome, but it’s not fluency. And when the 30 days are up and you still can’t watch your show, it’s easy to decide you’re bad at languages. You’re not. They’re selling dopamine, not results.

    Real progress doesn’t look like an ad. It looks like understanding one more sentence than last time, or recognizing a word you mined three weeks ago. If you’ve ever fallen for one of these, don’t beat yourself up.

    Find your people

    One of the biggest things that changed my journey was finding a community: people to talk to about the shows you’re watching and the words you just learned. It creates this camaraderie, study buddies who keep you honest and challenge you. The advanced stage especially is a long, quiet stretch, and having people around makes it a lot less lonely.

    That’s why our Discord exists: hundreds of learners at every level, with an anime club, a book club, and people posting their progress. Come hang out.

    The advanced stretch

    Near the top, you stop feeling progress from one day to the next. That’s normal, and it’s one of the longest parts of the journey. The answer isn’t a new method. It’s the same loop: find stuff to immerse in, make cards, review, immerse. Pick harder content when things get comfy. Do your hobbies in Japanese. Keep coming back.

    Don’t stress. You’ve already come this far. Just keep pushing and come back to it every day. You will make it, inevitably.

    — me, filmed outside in sub-zero weather, shivering

    要TL;DR

    • Know your burnout signs. Catch yourself chasing numbers.
    • Sleep. It’s literally when you learn.
    • One non-negotiable hour; short, consistent sessions.
    • Compare yourself only to past you.
    • There’s no shortcut to buy. Find people to do it with.
