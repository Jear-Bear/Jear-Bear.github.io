    はじめに

    Who I am, what AJATT actually means, the one loop this whole guide is built on, and why there is no rush.

    8 min readEveryone✓ Read

    始

    Hey, I’m Jared. 8 years ago I got into Japanese because I wanted to be the guy who could watch anime without subtitles. I thought that would be so awesome.

    Then I spent six years doing it the “normal” way: high school classes, Genki 1 and 2, a college course, Tobira, a pile of vocab lists. I genuinely thought I was fluent. Then I went to Japan and could barely scrape by on surface-level daily Japanese. I was crushed.

    So about two years ago I restarted with AJATT, and it’s the reason I can now read novels, play games, joke around with people in VRChat, and talk to my host family like an actual human being. This guide is everything I learned along the way, including all the dumb mistakes, so you can skip them.

    4,000+hours spent with Japanese
    1,000 hrsof reading, my goal for this year
    30books, also this year (mostly Ascendance of a Bookworm, no regrets)
    < 10%of my Japanese time is spent in flashcards

    What even is AJATT?

    AJATT stands for All Japanese All The Time. You might also hear MIA (Mass Immersion Approach) or just “immersion.” The idea is simple: instead of studying about Japanese, you spend as much of your life as possible in Japanese. Anime, YouTube, games, manga, novels, podcasts, your phone, your group chats. You surround yourself with it until you’re basically living in Japan, from your bedroom.

    The internet has made this ridiculously easy. You don’t need to drop $2,000 on a flight to get input. You can build your own little Japan at home and tailor it to exactly what you like and exactly your level, which is arguably better than being dropped in the real thing with no control.

    If getting fluent was as simple as watching a bunch of anime, every weeb on Earth would be cracked at Japanese. Myself included.

    — me, looking at my MyAnimeList

    But “just immerse, bro” isn’t the whole story either. Immersion works when you pair it with a couple of simple tools and you pick content that’s actually at your level. That’s what most of this guide is about.

    The whole method fits in one loop

    My friend Hypothetical Tiger put it best: immerse → make cards from your immersion → rep your cards → repeat until fluent. Scroll through it:

    1 · Immerse 浸

    Watch, read, play, and listen to Japanese stuff you genuinely enjoy, at a level where you understand most of it.

    2 · Mine 掘

    When a sentence has just one word you don’t know, save it as a flashcard. Two clicks with the right tools.

    3 · Review 復

    Your flashcard app shows you those cards right before you’d forget them. Ten or twenty minutes a day.

    4 · Repeat 又

    You understand more, so harder content opens up, so you find new words, so… you get it. That’s the whole thing.

    There’s one more rule underneath the loop: you get better at the thing you actually do. Want to read better? Read more. Want to understand anime? Watch more anime. Want to speak? At some point, you have to speak. I used to be a swimmer. You know how I got faster? I went to swim practice.

    Every word is one more stone

    The way I like to picture it: every word and kanji you learn is a stone on your own personal Japanese mountain. Some days you add a few, some days a lot, some days none. It doesn’t matter. The mountain only goes up.

    (The stone at the top is 凪, nagi: a dead calm at sea. It’s my favorite kanji.)

    It’s not a race

    This is the single most important thing I want you to get out of this guide, and it took me the longest to learn.

    You’ll see videos promising “fluent in 3 months.” You’ll see people who got “fluent in 8 months” and wonder why you’re 4 years in and still struggling. I’ve been there. That grindset mindset is exactly what made me quit Japanese four or five times.

    Here’s some math: a human lifetime is roughly 700,000 hours. Getting comfortable in Japanese takes something like 2,200 hours for an English speaker, and many more to get near-native. What’s the rush? If you put in time consistently, you will hit whatever that number is for you. It’s inevitable.

    Why go fast when you could have fun?

    — me, ranting on a very hot day

    I think of it like driving through a city you’ve never seen. You could floor it to your destination, or you could take your time, enjoy the scenery, and come out the other side with a lot more to show for it. Japanese is the scenery. I’m doing this for the love of the game.

    話Story time

    I work a full-time cybersecurity job. I edit a YouTube video every week, upload shorts, run a Discord. Some of the people getting “fluent in a year” are students or live with their parents and have all day to study. I don’t have that luxury, and you might not either. Once I accepted that it would take me longer, I stopped quitting.

    How this guide works

    • Chapters go in order for a total beginner, but every chapter stands on its own. Use the chapter list (left side, or the Chapters button on your phone) to jump around.
    • Your progress saves in your browser. Scroll to the end of a chapter (or hit the button) and it gets a check mark.
    • Everything you can click, click. The widgets aren’t decoration; they make the point better than I can in text.
    • I link my videos where they go deeper, but you never need them to follow along.
    • I tell you what I actually do, including where I disagree with other people. Steal what works, ignore the rest.

    要TL;DR

    • AJATT = live your life in Japanese, as much as you can, with stuff you enjoy.
    • The loop: immerse → mine → review → repeat.
    • You get better at what you actually do.
    • It’s not a race. Consistency beats intensity, every time.
