    Kanji without the wall

    漢字

    Two thousand-ish characters look like a wall. Learn them through words, in context, and they turn into a toolbox instead.

    7 min readBeginner → Intermediate✓ Read

    字

    Kanji are the thing that scares the most people away from Japanese. You hear that there are 2,136 of them and that you need to know them all to read anything, and that’s a big number. My friend SevenStop, who later got a near-perfect score on the N1, said that when he started, his first thought was “I’m not even going to learn kanji, that seems hard.” But you don’t actually learn kanji the way that number makes it sound. You learn words, and the kanji come along with them.

    Learn them in context

    The classic approach is to grind kanji one at a time: the meaning, every on’yomi and kun’yomi, the radicals, all in isolation. It falls apart pretty quickly once you meet real words. Take 行, or tap through these:

    If you learn those as words instead, each one comes with its own reading, its own meaning and a sentence you remember it from, so you never have to stop and pick which reading to use. You just know 銀行 is ぎんこう because you’ve seen it a hundred times. Kanji also have nuance, just like words, so knowing the meanings of two kanji doesn’t always tell you what they mean together. I’d rather spend those hours immersing and picking kanji up inside real words than memorizing facts in a vacuum.

    Your starter deck already does a lot of this, and every word you mine adds more. After a while the parts start repeating: 氵 (water) in 海, 泳 and 洗, or 青 sounding like せい in 晴, 清 and 精. DASmallWorlds calls the point where this snowballs a critical mass, where you start guessing the reading and meaning of new words from kanji you already know.

    What about RTK?

    Full transparency, I did Remembering the Kanji myself, kind of late. I could understand most easy anime but my reading was so bad, and I kept mixing up look-alike kanji because each one was just a lump of lines to me. So I made a physical box of flashcards and did 20 kanji a day: write them on cards, review the paper two days later, into Anki five days after that. It took about six months, and watching the box fill up was weirdly motivating.

    What actually made them stick, though, was running into the same kanji that day in a visual novel or a chat with a friend. Later I suspended a lot of those RTK cards and only turned each one back on when I ran into that kanji in the wild. So would I start with RTK if I were beginning today? Probably not. Learning words in context gets you the same “oh, that’s 氵 plus 羊” recognition naturally, and you spend the time actually reading Japanese. But if seeing kanji as parts would help you, or you just enjoy kanji as a hobby, it’s a fine tool, and RRTK (the recognition-only version, where you look at a kanji and go “yep, that means stop” instead of writing it out) is a lighter way to do it. SevenStop’s take is that to really acquire kanji you have to see them in lots of different words through immersion, and that it’s not a race. I agree with him.

    The genuinely hard parts

    I won’t pretend it’s all easy; if you asked me the hardest part of Japanese, I’d cheat and say kanji and pitch accent. What still gets me is words that sound the same, like 後悔 (こうかい, regret) and 公開 (こうかい, publishing something, like a video). “I regret publishing that video” is a sentence I’ve thought more than once, and it’s the kanji and the pitch accent that tell them apart. Then there are the weird readings: 行う, 行方, or お開き, which uses the kanji for “open” to mean the end of a wedding or banquet. What the heck? Those are really just exposure, though. You see them enough and they stop being weird.

    If you get into this stuff, I built a Kanji trainer and a 難読 board of hard-to-read kanji on this site for when kanji turns into a hobby instead of a hurdle. As for writing them by hand, that’s optional and depends on your goals (see chapter 3). Reading and writing are separate skills, and you can read thousands and write fifty like me.
