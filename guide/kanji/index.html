    Kanji without the wall

    漢字

    Two thousand-ish characters look like a wall. They’re not. Learn them through words, in context, and they turn into a toolbox.

    8 min readBeginner → Intermediate✓ Read

    字

    Kanji are the thing that scares people away from Japanese. “You need to learn 2,136 characters before you can read anything.” My friend SevenStop, who later got a near-perfect score on the N1, said that when he started he thought, “I’m not even going to learn kanji. That seems hard.”

    Here’s the thing: you don’t learn kanji the way that number makes it sound. You learn words, and the kanji come along for the ride.

    Learn kanji in context, not in isolation

    The classic approach is to grind kanji one by one: the meaning, every on’yomi, every kun’yomi, the radicals. It falls apart fast. Take 行. Tap the cards:

    If you learn those as words, each one comes with its own reading, its own meaning and a sentence you remember it from, and you never have to “pick” which reading to use. Over time, you just know that 銀行 is ぎんこう because you’ve seen it a hundred times.

    Kanji also have nuance, just like words. Knowing the meanings of two kanji doesn’t always tell you what they mean together. So I’d rather spend those thousands of hours immersing and learning kanji inside real words than memorizing facts in a vacuum.

    How that looks in practice

    • Your starter deck teaches kanji for you. Kaishi 1.5K introduces each word with its kanji, bit by bit. No separate kanji grind needed.
    • Every word you mine adds kanji. Look at them for a second when you review. Notice the parts.
    • The parts start to repeat. After a while you’ll notice 氵 (water) in 海, 泳, 洗, and that 青 tends to sound like せい in 晴, 清, 精. Nobody had to tell you.
    • It snowballs. DASmallWorlds calls it a critical mass: once you know enough kanji, you use kanji to learn new kanji. Most words are built from a common set, so you can guess the reading and meaning of new ones. (If you’re curious, read up on semantic-phonetic kanji. It explains a lot.)

    What about RTK and radicals?

    Full transparency: I did Remembering the Kanji (RTK) myself, kind of late. I could already understand 80–90% of easy anime, but my reading was so bad. I could recognize around 1,500 kanji, but I kept mixing up the ones that look alike, because to me each one was just a lump of lines. So I made a physical box of flashcards and did 20 kanji a day: write them on cards that day, review the paper cards two days later, and five days after first seeing them they went into Anki. It took about six months. It helped me see kanji as parts instead of scribbles, and the box full of written cards was weirdly motivating.

    The part that actually made them stick, though, was seeing the same kanji that day in a visual novel or a chat with a friend. Later I suspended my RTK cards and only turned each one back on when I ran into that kanji in the wild.

    Would I tell you to do it today? No. If you learn words in context, you get the same “oh, that’s 氵 plus 羊” recognition naturally, and you spend that time actually reading Japanese. If you genuinely enjoy kanji as a hobby (some people love it), go wild. Otherwise, words first.

    友SevenStop’s take

    “To really acquire kanji, you have to use your unconscious mind and go through immersion and see lots of different words. And it’s not a race either. Just take it slow, learn words, and you can learn kanji along with vocab as you go.”

    The genuinely hard parts

    I won’t pretend it’s all easy. Two things took me forever:

    • Words that sound the same. 後悔 (こうかい, regret) and 公開 (こうかい, publishing a video). “I regret publishing that video” is a sentence I’ve thought more than once. The kanji (and the pitch accent!) are what tell them apart.
    • Weird readings. お開き above, or 行方, or all the different ways to read 生. These are just exposure. You see them enough, they stop being weird.

    If you like this stuff, I built a Kanji trainer and a 難読 board of hard-to-read kanji on this site, for when kanji becomes a hobby and not just a hurdle.

    And writing them?

    Optional, depending on your goals (see chapter 2). Reading kanji and writing kanji are separate skills. You can read thousands and write fifty, like me. If you enjoy it or think it’s cool, it does help with telling look-alikes apart, so go for it.

    Video 5 Japanese Language Myths That Are Holding You Back (with SevenStop)

    要TL;DR

    • Learn words, not kanji in isolation. The kanji come with them.
    • Notice the parts as you review; the patterns show up on their own.
    • RTK is optional. I did it; I wouldn’t start with it now.
    • Writing by hand: optional, but fun if you’re into it.
