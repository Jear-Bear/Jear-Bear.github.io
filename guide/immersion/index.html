    Immersion that actually works

    没入

    “Just immerse, bro” is half right. Picking content at the right level, active vs passive listening, subtitles, my YouTube tier list, and the video games that are secretly great teachers.

    12 min readEveryone✓ Read

    浸

    “Just immerse, bro.” You’ve seen it on r/LearnJapanese. Some dude in a Discord server has said it to you. At this point it’s the holy gospel of language learning, and it’s mostly right! But it took me years of setbacks to figure out the parts nobody says out loud. That’s this chapter.

    Is it immersion, though?

    Not everything with Japanese in it counts. Here are a few situations I’ve been in. Guess which ones were doing anything:

    Picking what to immerse in

    The biggest skill in immersion is picking what to immerse in. Too hard, and you drown in unknown words and you’re mentally fried in ten minutes. Too easy, and you don’t grow. Here’s roughly what 80% comprehension feels like:

    For shows, anything above about 70% is enough to enjoy it, and at 85 or 90% you’re just chilling and picking up the rest from context. You can check that number on jpdb.io for almost any anime, manga, novel or game. But I weigh it against two other things. Interest: sorry to any med students, but a super easy medical textbook in Japanese still loses to a horror story for me. And difficulty, which works like the gym. Lift the same weight forever and you don’t get stronger.

    Something that helped me a lot this year: researching stuff before I start it. Even if everyone says a show is “goated for immersion,” I ask myself, could I sit through dozens of hours of this and come out happy? Five minutes of reading about it gets me hyped, and hype carries you a long way.

    Then there’s the comfort trap. When I could sit through Komi Can’t Communicate and enjoy every minute, that was my sign to level up. So I keep a list of harder stuff I want to watch, like Psycho-Pass and Detective Conan, and move up to it. My old swim coach used to say you’re not going to grow if you don’t put yourself under pressure. He was right.

    Active and passive immersion

    Active immersion is when Japanese has your full attention: Japanese subtitles on, pausing and replaying lines, looking things up, mining. Most bang for your buck. Also tiring. Passive immersion is Japanese playing while you do something else, and it gets a bad rep. Partly because of people like me, blasting Japanese radio while scrolling Instagram in English and calling it immersion.

    My friend SevenStop explained it in a way that changed how I do it. You still pay attention during passive immersion. You just do it while your hands run on autopilot: driving, mowing the lawn, making breakfast. Homework doesn’t count, since that needs your brain. It also depends on your level. As a beginner, the best passive listening is stuff you’ve already watched actively, like ripping the audio from an anime episode and putting it on your phone. Once your listening is around intermediate, you can learn a ton from new podcasts and YouTube in the background, the same way you’d follow an English podcast in the car.

    Subtitles

    Japanese subtitles give you more context, confirm what you heard, and let you look things up and mine as you go (chapter 5 shows how to get them on almost anything). English subtitles mostly turn the Japanese into background noise. I binged all of Free!, the swimming anime, with English subs, told myself it still counted as exposure, and came out knowing… the character names. What did help: watching the shows I couldn’t wait for in English first, then coming back to them in Japanese once I knew the plot.

    My YouTube tier list

    I graded YouTube channels on whether they’re comprehensible, whether it’s natural Japanese, whether I’d actually binge them, and whether they get you closer to native content:

    SSS

    Chill art & cozy vlogs

    The kind of slop I could consume all day (and I mean that nicely). Native, calm, clear, and healing for the heart.

    Gaming, food, documentaries

    Let’s Players are my favorite. I shadow mine like he’s my language parent. Food content has endless visuals to help you guess words. Documentaries teach specialized vocab once you’re upper-intermediate.

    “How to learn Japanese” channels

    The creators are S-tier, but the genre isn’t Japanese, so it can’t be S. Watch them to improve your process, then go immerse. Don’t binge them instead of immersing.

    Variety YouTubers

    Great for figuring out what you actually like in Japanese. In English I watch internet-drama breakdowns; in Japanese my algorithm led me to tech and gaming. Fast, slangy, intermediate and up.

    Hugely important as a beginner, but it’s a gate, not a destination. Real people talk faster, drop words, and use way more casual speech. CI channels once fed my ego so much that I thought I was fluent. Then I studied abroad.

    What about anime?

    A lot of people worry that learning from anime will make them sound like an anime character. SevenStop spent his first year and a half or so immersing mostly in anime, and he doesn’t sound weird at all. Probably 95% of anime dialogue is just normal Japanese. If a character says something wild, ask yourself, would a normal person say that? And branch out into vlogs, dramas and podcasts. It helps your vocabulary too. If I only watched detective shows, I’d miss politics, fantasy words, cooking, all of it.

    Video games count

    I used to feel so guilty every time I picked up a controller. “You haven’t finished your Anki. You could be reading.” Then I had a shocking revelation. Games come in Japanese. So I tested nine of them for an hour each, tracking how much Japanese I saw, how much I understood and how many words I mined:

    A visual novel throws around 25,000 characters an hour at you, while Resident Evil 3 gives you about 9,000, because you’re busy running from zombies. Visual novels are the gold mine, but I’m an open-world junkie, and Cyberpunk 2077 has Japanese everywhere, from phone calls to random people on the street. And even DayZ, the worst one on my list, beats lying in bed thinking about immersing.

    To mine from games, I use Game Sentence Miner with OBS. GSM grabs the text off the screen, Yomitan works on it, and your cards come with a screenshot and the voice line. Set OBS’s replay buffer to about three minutes, and mute your mic and desktop audio in OBS so your Discord call doesn’t end up on your flashcards.

    One more trick: binge one genre for a while. I went through a run of horror detective games that all share the same words about ghosts, curses and crime scenes, and got a ton of little “aha” moments when they popped up again in the next one. So here’s your homework, and it’s a fun one: look up one show or game you’ve been meaning to play on jpdb, and if it’s in that 70 to 90% range, start it tonight.
