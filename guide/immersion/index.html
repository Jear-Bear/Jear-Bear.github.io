    Immersion that actually works

    没入

    “Just immerse, bro” is half right. How to pick content at the right level, active vs passive listening, subtitles, my YouTube tier list, and which video games are secretly the best teachers.

    14 min readEveryone✓ Read

    浸

    “Just immerse, bro.” You’ve seen it on r/LearnJapanese, you’ve heard it from some dude in a Discord server. It’s become the holy gospel of language learning. And it’s… mostly right. But it took me years of setbacks to figure out the parts nobody says out loud.

    Is it immersion, though?

    Pick content you’d watch anyway, at the right level

    The single most important skill in immersion is choosing what to immerse in. Too hard and you drown in unknown words and your mental stamina runs out in ten minutes. Too easy and you don’t grow. Here’s how 80% comprehension actually feels:

    People throw around numbers like “you need 98% comprehension.” For me there’s no magic number, but anything above about 70–80% is enough to enjoy something. Three things I balance:

    1. Interest. An easy book about something you don’t care about loses to a harder one you can’t put down. If someone wrote a super easy medical textbook in Japanese, I still wouldn’t read it. Give me horror or a murder mystery.
    2. Difficulty. Like the gym: if you lift the same weight forever, you don’t get stronger. Keep adding a little.
    3. Comprehension. Somewhere in that 70–95% window. Check jpdb.io for any anime, manga, novel or game before you commit.

    One extra step that helped me a ton: research things before you start them. Even if everyone says a show is “goated for immersion,” maybe it’s not for you. Ask yourself: could I sit down for dozens of hours with this and come out happy? That little bit of research gets me hyped for it, which makes the whole thing way more fun.

    注The comfort trap

    When you can sit through something like Komi Can’t Communicate and enjoy it fully, that’s a sign to level up, not to stay. I keep a list of harder stuff I genuinely want to watch (Psycho-Pass, Detective Conan) and move up to it. As my swim coach used to say: you’re not going to grow if you don’t put yourself under pressure.

    Active vs passive immersion

    Active immersion is when Japanese has your full attention: you’re watching with Japanese subtitles, pausing, replaying lines, looking things up, mining. It’s the most bang for your buck, and it’s tiring. Do it when you have the energy.

    Passive immersion is Japanese playing while you do something else. It gets a bad rep, but here’s the thing my friend SevenStop explained that changed how I do it:

    友SevenStop’s take

    Passive immersion isn’t about not paying attention. It’s about paying attention while doing something that runs on autopilot, like driving, mowing the lawn, or making breakfast. Not homework or math. And it depends on your level: as a beginner, relisten to stuff you’ve already watched actively (rip the audio from an anime episode and put it on your phone). Once your listening is around intermediate, you can learn tons from new podcasts and YouTube in the background, the same way you follow an English podcast while driving.

    Listen with intent. When I’m actively watching, I keep Japanese subtitles on, pause on lines that stand out, replay them, shadow them out loud, and mine anything that’s i+1. When I’m in the car, I put on something I’ve already seen, or a podcast at my level.

    Subtitles: Japanese, not English

    • Japanese subtitles are one of the best things you can do. They give you more context, confirm what you heard, and let you mine in real time. Chapter 4 shows how to get them on anything.
    • English subtitles mostly turn Japanese into background noise. One trick that does work: watch a show in English first if you really want to see it now, and put it on a list to rewatch in Japanese later. You’ll know the plot, which makes the Japanese way easier.
    • No subtitles: great listening practice once you’re comfortable, especially for stuff you’ve seen before.

    Anime: yes, you can learn from it

    A common worry: “If I learn from anime, I’ll sound like an anime character.” SevenStop spent his first year and a half to two years immersing mostly in anime and doesn’t sound weird at all. Most of your time goes into acquiring the basic building blocks and the nuance of super common words, and those are the same whether you learn them from anime or the news. Probably 95%+ of even anime is just normal Japanese.

    Just don’t only watch anime, and use your brain. If a character says something wild, ask yourself, “Would a normal person say this?” Branch out into vlogs, dramas, YouTube and podcasts, and you’ll naturally pick up what’s normal and what’s anime.

    Branching out matters for vocabulary too. If I only watched detective shows, I’d miss politics, isekai fantasy words, cooking words, all of it. Mix genres.

    Video games: guilt-free immersion

    I used to feel guilty every time I played a game. “You haven’t finished your Anki. You could be reading.” Then I had the shocking revelation that games come in Japanese. So I tested nine of them for an hour each, tracking how much Japanese I saw, how much I understood, and how many words I mined:

    What I learned:

    • Genre decides how much Japanese you get. Visual novels throw 25,000 characters an hour at you; an action game like Resident Evil 3 gives you 9,000, because you’re busy running from zombies.
    • Visual novels are the gold mine. Every genre, every difficulty level, and you control the reading speed.
    • Open-world games are my personal favorite. Cyberpunk 2077 has Japanese everywhere: prompts, phone calls, even random people on the street. It never felt like studying.
    • Even DayZ, the worst one on the list, is infinitely better than lying in bed thinking about immersing and never actually doing it.

    To mine from games, use Game Sentence Miner + OBS. GSM grabs the text off the screen (OCR), Yomitan works on it, and your cards come with a screenshot and the line’s audio. Set OBS’s replay buffer to about 3 minutes, and mute your mic and desktop audio in OBS so your Discord call doesn’t end up on your flashcards.

    One more trick: binge one genre. I went through a run of horror detective games, and they all share the same pile of words: ghosts, curses, crime scenes. You get tons of “aha” moments when those words show up again in the next game, or in a horror anime like Mob Psycho. And if you want to see what playing a game for Japanese actually looks like, I have a series where I go through games line by line with a dictionary open.

    要TL;DR

    • Pick things you’d enjoy anyway, at roughly 70–95% comprehension. Check jpdb.io.
    • Active when you have energy, passive during autopilot tasks. Beginners: relisten.
    • Japanese subtitles on, English subtitles off.
    • Mix genres. Anime is fine; anime-only isn’t.
    • Games count. Visual novels especially.
